## Preface

Linear A is attested on some 1,700 documents, of which the greater part are administrative, and it has not been read. Proposals to read it are numerous and are not comparable with one another: findings circulate by citation, and nothing separates the claims that have been tested from those that have only been repeated.

KU-RO is the Minoan word for "total". This book counts.

It does not decipher Linear A. Not one phonetic value is fixed here that was not already known. What it does is measure, against explicit null models throughout, what can be established about the script, say what the corpus cannot settle and what settling it would cost, and record what did not work.

It is organised as an argument rather than a collection. Part One lets the corpus describe itself: the archive and the formula, without judging. Part Two tries to read it, and fails; the failure is recorded. Part Three asks what it would take, and answers with a number. Part Four says where that leaves the question. Part Five holds what came after the rest was written.

Three independent routes give the same figure for what is missing: a deficit of 345 bits, which is 56 signs to anchor from outside, or some 788 further units of intact text. Seven walls are named with their figures, and none is removed by computation. Five require excavation or specialist transcription; two require a find.

A word on how this was written. Sixteen apparent findings of this project turned out to be already published; each was caught by a three-minute search, and each is credited to its author in the text. Six further claims were positive results of this work that a control destroyed, among them a z of +6.88 for Akkadian that proved to be editorial marks in the sign skeletons. Both kinds are recorded rather than removed.

The corpus, the code, the null models and the manifest holding every figure asserted here are public. The manifest names, for each claim, the condition that would refute it.

# Part One. The corpus speaks about itself

*Two studies that describe without judging: what the largest Minoan archive counts and how it seals, and what the votive formula does. Nothing here requires a reading; everything is measured against a null.*

## 1. The Haghia Triada archive and its sealing

### Summary

What a Minoan archive does can be established without reading it. This chapter measures the accounting documents of Haghia Triada and the sealing system that accompanies them, against explicit null models throughout.

Part I treats the tablets: what recurs, how the account is organised, what each commodity's metrology is, and what the archive does not record. Part II treats the roundels and nodules: two distinguishable sealing circuits, their iconography, and the vocabulary that barely overlaps with the accounts.

Claims already in the literature are measured rather than repeated. Three of Schoep's hold in a narrower sense than stated; the entry groups of Packard and of Uchitel and Finkelberg are confirmed against a null for the first time.

Keywords: Linear A, Haghia Triada, Minoan administration, sealing, roundels, null models.

## Part I. The accounting documents

The following work bears directly on what is measured here and is credited accordingly: A search of the NESTOR bibliography returns work on this archive that bear directly on what is measured here and are credited accordingly: Montecchi 2019 (*Contare a Haghia Triada: le tavolette in lineare A, i noduli*), a monograph on counting in this very archive; Montecchi 2010, a classification proposal for its tablets, and Montecchi 2012 on banqueting lists; Schrijver 2014, "Fractions and food rations in Linear A" (*Kadmos* 53), and Facchetti 2012 on metrograms J and E, both peer-reviewed treatments of the metrology measured here; Uchitel 2006 on listing by age and occupation; and eight papers by Tomas (2008-2023) comparing Linear A and Linear B administration, which bear on the transition measurements. The nulls of this chapter stand; the attribution does not.

#### Summary

Haghia Triada preserves the largest administrative archive of Linear A: 1,108 of the 1,720 documents of the corpus. This chapter describes it as an archive and not as a language, under a single procedure: every claim about what a unit does is fixed by distribution against a permutation null with a measured error rate, and filed with the evidence for, the evidence against, and a refutation condition. Six results. First, the anatomy of the Minoan receipt compared with six Bronze Age administrations: the same five functions and two absences, with no transmission required. Second, the functional inventory, which rises from 65 units fixed one by one to 151 once a class-level function is admitted: the first syllabic unit of a tablet takes a figure 29% of the time against 72% for all others (p = 8·10⁻³⁴), so the opening position is the label of the record and not an entry in the account. Third, five tablet formats identified by motif discovery against a Markov null, among them the transaction template, which the procedure recovers unprompted. Fourth, the list entries, the most numerous class and until now the one without an instrument, fall into four stable groups that recur together across documents (35 pairs against 8.2 under the null), one of which Uchitel and Finkelberg set out by inspection in 1995 while the other three are a different partition of the same material: they are sets of membership and not sequences, and one of them receives grain in multiples of ten with the most regular quantities in the corpus. Fifth, allocation is modular: eight of 119 tablets have every figure a multiple of the smallest (null 1.9) and fifty-one repeat one figure three or more times (null 36.0), which describes allocation by quota rather than an inventory of stock. Sixth, the inventory declares its scope: 51 of the 65 readings fixed one by one are fixed on the accounting archive, 7 on the votive inscriptions and 4 on the sealings, and a claim fixed on one genre does not transfer to another. Seventh, the two classes of sign are bound by a rule: the syllabogram ligatured to a commodity logogram belongs to that commodity in 16 of 26 cases against 25% expected by chance (p < 0.0005), which is the acrophonic device Linear B uses, and the ten that circulate are precisely the entries this inventory had filed as general qualifiers. Eighth, each commodity has its own metrology: grain is counted in tens (34% multiples of ten against a base rate of 17%) while oil, olives and cyperus fall below that rate, which accounts for the archive of Khania, dominated by cyperus, never summing (0 of 105 tablets against 41 of 205) and recording in fractions. Three facts about the corpus accompany all of this: 276 of 783 syllabic units are broken in every attestation; the archive records no metal and no weaponry, with 1 of 1,480 logogram occurrences against 14.3% in Linear B; and the ceiling of what the internal method fixes with this corpus is functions, not words.

Keywords: Linear A, Haghia Triada, Neopalatial administration, functional inventory, metrology, null models, class-level functions.

#### Introduction: an archive that is understood and a language that is not read

What is known of Linear A with certainty fits in a sentence: it is the script of a Cretan administration of the sixteenth century BC that counted grain, oil, wine, figs, livestock and people, and added them up. What is not known is what any of it was called. Between those two things lies a space the field has filled with proposed readings and that this chapter fills with something else: a measured description of the archive, unit by unit, with what each one does and what would refute it.

The paper has six parts. The first (§1) asks what shape a Minoan receipt has and how it resembles and differs from those of Susa, Uruk, Ebla, Ugarit, Pylos and Knossos. The second (§2) builds the inventory of units with a fixed function and declares the three facts about the corpus that the editions do not state. Two further parts (§2 bis, §2 ter) treat the formats of the tablet and the internal structure of the list entries. The fifth (§3) descends to one file, the aromatics, where the procedure produces class readings and an identification under dispute. The sixth (§4) compares the archive of Khania with that of Haghia Triada and shows they are not the same archive at different sizes. The discussion (§5) states what is fixed, what remains open, and why the number of functions stops where it does.

### 1.1 The anatomy of the receipt

#### 1.1.1 The question

That Minoan writing arises for accounting is not disputed; whether its form is native or learned is. Crete traded with the Levant and with Egypt from the Protopalatial period, its products appear at Ugarit and at Amarna, and from the East come goods whose names Greek later inherited through Minoan (sesame, cumin). The transmission of goods and of words is documented. Was the form of the document transmitted too?

The question can be answered without reading Minoan, because the form of an archive is measurable: which administrative functions it puts into words, and how often. This work measures it in six archives and answers no.

#### 1.1.2 Method and data

For each corpus the closed class is extracted: the lexical units that recur across many documents and are neither proper names nor commodities. In the lemmatised corpora the class is read from the glossary with its instance counts; in the unlemmatised ones the forms occurring in a minimum number of documents, and across several sites where there is more than one. Each term is then assigned to one of five administrative functions: total and account; transfer (give, receive, dispatch, by the hand of); responsibility and seal; date; measure. The assignment is the field's own in the read corpora, and ours by distribution in Linear A, where three of the terms are additionally fixed by arithmetic.

Corpora: Linear A (GORILA [1] via the LinearA Explorer, 1,098 word occurrences on tablets, 782 types); TCMA, Archival Texts of the Middle Assyrian Period (ORACC, 2,276 tablets, 89,677 lemmatised instances, 2,524 lemmas); The Amarna Letters (ORACC, 305 texts, 29,961 instances); SAA 7 (ORACC, 219 texts, 8,574 instances); BDTNS for Ur III (20,000 tablets sampled from 101,389, 432,915 tokens); CDLI for proto-Elamite (1,594 tablets) and Uruk IV-III (6,387).

#### 1.1.3 The anatomy is common

All six archives write the entry as unit plus quantity, open with a header, and close with a total in some documents. The proportion of entries with a quantity runs from 0.60 to 0.80. The partition of the lexicon is the same in all: a short class of frequent repeated terms, which are the commodities and the functions, and a long class of unique units, which are the names and places. At Susa, one-sign strings are singletons in 3% of cases and strings of three or more in 87-99%; at Haghia Triada 76% of the vocabulary is singleton; in Ur III and Middle Assyrian the proportion is of the same order once proper names, which both corpora mark explicitly, are discounted.

That anatomy occurs in proto-Elamite and at Uruk, fifteen hundred years and two thousand kilometres from Crete, with no relation between Susa and Crete. It is the shape a record of deliveries takes when it is written down, and it requires no transmission.

The identification of the Minoan terms is not a contribution of this work. Schoep [2] (2002) established them with their function: TE, separated by dividers on both sides and always at the head of a list with one or more logograms; KU-RO, "total", followed as in Linear B by the sum of the preceding entries; PO-TO-KU-RO, "grand total"; KI-RO, only at Haghia Triada, as deficit, on the argument that when it follows a KU-RO it is always smaller; and SA-RA₂, also exclusive to Haghia Triada, whose mutual exclusion with TE within mixed-commodity tablets suggests the two represent opposite transactions. What is contributed here is not the inventory of terms but the comparison of functional repertoires across six archives.

Two observations. The first concerns coverage: the twenty most frequent units cover 47.6% of the Middle Assyrian corpus, 52.2% of Amarna, 28.3% of Ur III and 13.7% of Linear A. The low Cretan figure does not indicate a poorer functional vocabulary but that commodities there are written with logograms and not with words. Comparing like with like, the closed class is of the same order in all six: between fifteen and twenty-five terms.

The second is what matters. In the nearest contemporary archive, transfer and date are not marginal functions: "give" and "take" together exceed a thousand instances, the year eponym occurs 1,849 times and the day 2,599, which is to say almost every tablet is dated. In Linear A there is no word that behaves as a verb of transfer or as a date. And the absence is not one of identification: of the twenty words that make up the closed class of Linear A, all have a function assigned by position or are frequent names; none is left without a destination that could be a "received" or a "year".

#### 1.1.5 The Cretan practice

It delegates both functions to the object and to the cycle.

The seal does the work of the verb. Haghia Triada produces hundreds of nodules and roundels against a modest number of tablets; the roundel carries a sign group, sometimes a commodity logogram, and as many seal impressions as units. It is a complete receipt with no word of transfer: who delivers is in the sign group, what is delivered in the logogram, how much in the impressions, and before whom in the seal, which identifies the receiving official and not the party. Middle Assyrian, which also seals, writes the word *kunukku* 789 times besides; Crete seals and does not write it.

The cycle does the work of the date. An archive without dates cannot keep multi-year accounts or justify deliveries to third parties: it records the current cycle. That agrees with what archaeology says of Minoan archives, which were remade and recycled and not kept from one year to the next, and with Haghia Triada being the photograph of a single year, the year of the destruction, written by twenty-one scribes working at once.

The same asymmetry appears in a different genre and confirms it. The seven perfume recipes of the Assur archive write the procedure in full: light the fire, cover the bowl, let it stand overnight, sieve at dawn, discard the residue, and repeat the pouring counting the times. The Minoan tablets of the aromatics file at Haghia Triada record the same ingredients with the same structure of quantities and write not one step of the procedure. Assyria writes what is done; Crete, what comes in. The parallel extends to the genre closest to it: PY Un 267 records the delivery of ingredients to the perfumer and writes the verb of delivery, the name of the deliverer, the name and profession of the recipient and the purpose, while the corresponding Minoan tablets write none of those four things.

#### 1.1.6 Discussion

What the comparison allows one to state. The anatomy of the receipt is universal in the trivial sense: it is the shape any record of deliveries takes, and it appears in administrations without contact. What distinguishes traditions is which functions they verbalise, and there Crete does not resemble the Eastern model precisely in the two functions that model puts in the foreground. Had Minoan accounting been learned from the Levant or Mesopotamia, we should expect to find transfer and date written, because they are the most frequent words of the model; their systematic absence points to native development on a common anatomy.

What it does not allow. It does not say that Crete was ignorant of Eastern practice: the Minoans traded with people who dated and wrote "received", and could have known the practice and not adopted it, which is a decision and not an ignorance. Nor does it say anything about the language: the form of the document and the language it is written in are independent. And it rests on a small Minoan corpus; the claim that holds is about what the archive of Haghia Triada writes and does not write, and it would weaken if a Minoan archive with dates appeared.

What it suggests for the history of trade. The words that did travel to Crete from the East are commodity words (sesame, with its Semitic reduplication preserved; cumin), and those that travelled from Crete to Greece are commodity words too (cyperus, the sycomore fig, perhaps henna). The products and their names travelled, with the ships; the form did not. It is an informative asymmetry: Bronze Age trade transmitted goods and lexicon easily and administrative practice with difficulty, which fits administration being the domain where each palace protected its own way of doing things.

Two Syro-Levantine archives fall outside for want of machine-readable data, and this should be said precisely because they are the ones nearest to Crete. Alalakh has 771 objects catalogued in CDLI, 523 administrative and 294 from level IV, contemporary with Haghia Triada, but only three with available transliteration, and all three are lexical texts. A pending and decisive check is the archive of Ugarit, Crete's direct trading partner in the fourteenth century. With Ugarit measured, the conclusion would move from "the nearest contemporary archive" to "the archive of the trading partner", which is the strong test. This limitation is of the material and not of the method: the archives of Ugarit and Alalakh are treated here from published descriptions and not from the texts, since no digitised corpora of those archives exist in a form comparable to the others (J. Lauinger, personal communication, 9 September 2026).

### 1.2 The functional inventory

#### 1.2.1 The corpus and what the editions do not say about it

The corpus is GORILA through the LinearA Explorer: 1,720 documents and 1,007 distinct units, of which 156 have three or more attestations and at least one intact. That is the ceiling of the inventory: below three attestations there is no profile to measure.

The broken units. The `words` field of the edition preserves the breakage sign and the transliteration loses it. Recovered, 276 of the 783 syllabic units (35%) are broken in every attestation: ]MA-TE-RE is not MA-TE-RE. Three of the eight lexical coincidences with Linear B in a first comparison were fragments, and of Davis's root/ending pairs, five of eight endings fall below three roots once only intact units count (-JA, -ME and -TI remain). The whole inventory is computed over intact units.

The gaps. The material culture of Haghia Triada includes copper ingots, honey and fish. No sign with a figure has the profile of those things (low whole numbers or weight for metal, a liquid fraction for honey), \*118 is the weight unit and accompanies cyperus and sesame, and Younger's lexicon does not mention them. The tablet archive does not record them. The contrast with Linear B is sharp: 1 of 1,480 logogram occurrences against 14.3%.

#### 1.2.4 The route to the inventory

The inventory rose from 18 to 65 with the same procedure over the same units: the parallel with Linear B, the extrapolation of structures (qualifier, subtotal, header), name matching against a null, and two stages with a language model in the loop. Those two stages were measured: reading passages of the literature, the model invents non-existent units 22% of the time; proposing a reading from each unit's dossier, 1%. Its contribution was routing at scale, not the idea. A second pass over the remaining units found nothing.

That is the ceiling, and it should be stated precisely. It is not the ceiling of the problem: it is the ceiling of what can be verified with this corpus. The accounts have few functions and almost all are fixed; those that remain await an external datum; and the step from function to word requires a judge the corpus does not contain, because nothing in it can show that "KU-NI-SU means emmer" is false. That judge would be a bilingual or new running text, and those come out of the ground.

#### 1.2.5 The opening position: a function that belongs to a place, not to a unit

The inventory above was built unit by unit. That pace has an arithmetic ceiling: only 148 units have two or more intact attestations and only 68 have three or more, so most of the corpus will never assemble enough evidence on its own. There are, however, functions that belong not to a unit but to a position, and fixing the position classifies at once every unit that occupies it.

Comparing, over the tablets, the first intact syllabic unit of each with all the others:

|  | Units | With a figure following |
|---|---|---|
| The one that opens the tablet | 184 | **53 (29%)** |
| All others | 464 | 334 (72%) |

p = 8·10⁻³⁴. The opening position takes no quantity: it is not an entry in the account, it is the label of the record.

One hundred and forty-one distinct units occupy that position; ninety-five never take a figure there, and eighty-six of them had no function in the inventory. The nine that did, fixed one by one above (A-DU on eight tablets, SA-RA₂ on six, KA-PA on four, A-KA-RU, JE-DI and KI-RO on three, and three more), all fall inside the class: the procedure recovers what was already established and adds the rest. The inventory thus rises from 65 readings to 151.

Two cautions are filed with the result. The remaining 29%, the fifty-three openings that do take a figure, are not labels: the rule belongs to the position and not to each case, and each of the eighty-six inherits the function with the force of the class and not with that of an individual reading. And knowing that a unit labels a record does not say whether it names a place, an official, a month or an operation.

The other positions do not yield. Tested against the same base rate: the second unit (58% with a figure, p = 0.37), the third (65%, p = 0.88), the one that closes the tablet (59%, p = 0.47) and the one following an already-read unit (56%, p = 0.29). None departs from it. And one test was discarded as circular: "the unit preceding a logogram" gives 0 of 100 with a figure and a p of 3·10⁻⁴⁰, which means nothing, because if the next token is a logogram then by construction it is not a figure. The opening is the only class-level function this corpus sustains, and it is so because every tablet instantiates it exactly once over 184 tablets.

#### 1.2.6 The scope of the inventory: which genre each reading was fixed on

Part 4 states that the functions were fixed on Haghia Triada and weaken outside it. A second limit of the same kind has to be declared, and it came to light on reading Finkelberg [12] (2001).

The Linear A corpus holds at least three genres that do not behave alike: the accounting archive of tablets, the sealing system of nodules and roundels, and the votive inscriptions on stone vessels. Counted by the dominant genre of their attestations, the sixty-five readings fixed one by one divide as follows:

| Genre of the attestations | Readings |
|---|---|
| Accounting archive | **51** |
| Votive, on stone vessels | 7 |
| Sealing supports | 4 |
| Other | 2 |

Fourteen of the sixty-five therefore describe something other than the account, and among them are JA-SA-SA-RA-ME, U-NA-KA-NA-SI and DA-MA-TE, which belong to the libation formula, and TE, KU and KA, whose dominant use is on sealings.

The consequence beyond bookkeeping. Finkelberg (2001) builds a fourteen-point morphological profile of Minoan and concludes that the language is Anatolian, with Lycian as its descendant. Eight of her fourteen features rest on the sequence A-TA-I-\*301-WA-JA, whose eleven attestations are all on stone vessels. Her profile therefore describes the votive genre; this inventory describes the account. The two are not in competition over most of their extent, and saying so is not a concession but a statement of what each measures.

They do meet at one point. She reads *a-du* as the third person imperative of an Anatolian verb *a-* "to make"; this inventory has A-DU as a heading term on ten tablets. Measured, her paradigm, *ja-di* (1 attestation), *je-di* (4), *a-da* (1), *a-du* (10), *a-ra-u-da* (1), with *a-di* unattested, occupies the first syllabic position of its document in 12 of 17 attestations against a base rate of 0.405, p = 0.012. A verb does not systematically head its document; a record label does, and §2.6 measures that class at p = 8·10⁻³⁴. On this point the distributional reading holds, and it holds inside the archive, which is where both attestations of the disagreement lie.

#### 1.2.7 Picture and syllable in combination

The corpus has two classes of sign: the logogram that draws the commodity and the syllabogram that writes sound. Ligatures bind them, GRA+PA, OLE+KI, CYP+D, and this inventory has treated the two classes separately, measuring some as commodities and others as qualifiers. The relation between the classes had not been measured.

Thirty-nine distinct ligatures bind 26 syllabograms to 18 logograms. The question is whether the bound syllabogram belongs to its commodity or circulates among several: if it abbreviates something about that commodity it should be specific, and if it is a general grade mark it should circulate. The null reassigns the syllabograms to the logograms preserving how many ligatures each commodity carries, 2,000 repetitions.

|  | Observed | Null |
|---|---|---|
| Syllabograms bound to **one** commodity only | **16 of 26 (62%)** | 25% |
| Bound to two or more | 10 | 19.4 on average, minimum 15 |

p < 0.0005. The bound syllabogram goes with its own commodity far more than chance allows, which is what the acrophonic reading predicts: Linear B writes CYP+KU for *ku-pa-ro*, and the editors of the Knossos ivory inscriptions describe the same device for their vessels [6].

And the ten that do circulate are the ones this inventory had fixed. They are D, E, K, KA, KI, KU, MI, PA, QE and RA, and among them are precisely the entries filed above as general qualifiers: KU across grain, \*188 and textile; PA across \*304, cyperus and grain; KI across \*316 and oil; KA across wine and persons; RA across oil and wine. Those entries are measured one by one and stand, but their standing changes: they are the exceptions of a system whose rule is the opposite, and the inventory had recorded the minority without seeing the majority.

One reading error goes with the result. On first seeing the list of ten shared syllabograms the immediate conclusion was that the system was one of general grades and that acrophony did not hold. It was the reverse, and the null said so: ten shared looks like many until one sees that chance would give nineteen. A count without its null does not merely exaggerate an effect; it can invert its sign.

What this does not give is what any syllabogram abbreviates. That OLE+KI is specific to oil does not say what KI is, and no phonetic value is fixed by it. What is established is a property of the script: Minoan writes the type of a commodity by binding to its picture a syllable that belongs to that product, a rule of the writing system, measured, that requires reading nothing.

What the ligature is worth as a constraint on values, priced. If the bound syllabogram abbreviates something about its commodity, then its phonetic value is constrained: it must be the first syllable of a word in that commodity's field. That is a constraint on a sign's value obtained without comparing whole words, and no decipherment method uses it, because none looks at the logograms.

Nineteen syllabograms bind to a single commodity whose semantic field can be defined. Against the glossed lexicons of Akkadian, Ugaritic and Sumerian, each admits between 15 and 33 possible values under the acrophonic hypothesis, against the 74 a free sign admits. Under that hypothesis the constraint would be worth 36 bits of the 587 that a decipherment costs. It was tested on Linear B before being claimed, and it fails there. Where every value is known, the bound syllabogram is the first syllable of a word in the commodity's field in 2 cases of 10 (null 0.9, p = 0.22), and of a word adjacent to the logogram in 4 of 11 (null 3.23, p = 0.41). OLE+PA is *pa-ko-we* "sage-scented", OLE+A is *a-ro-pa* "unguent", CYP+KU is *ku-pa-ro*: each has its explanation and none follows a rule a model could apply without already knowing the answer. The distributional fact stands; the constraint on values does not, and the 36 bits are withdrawn.

### 1.3 The formats of the tablet

Motif discovery, which in bioinformatics locates conserved patterns with variable positions across few sequences without knowing what they mean, is applied here to administrative formats. Each tablet becomes a sequence of functional categories (W syllabic word, C commodity, N figure, f fraction, T total, L single sign, | divider) and recurrent n-grams are counted against a first-order Markov null preserving category frequencies and the transitions between them. 308 tablets, 80 runs of the null.

The positive control is supplied by the procedure itself: the motif `W | L | C N` occurs seven times against 0.48 expected, and it is the transaction template that §1 fixes by reading (SA-RO 𐄁 TE 𐄁 VIN, on HT 6a, 14, 17, 19, 21). The method recovers it unprompted, and extends it at both ends: the full template is party, divider, transaction term, divider, commodity, quantity.

Four further formats, none previously described as a genre:

|---|---|---|---|---|
| W f W f W f | 6 | 0.01 | HT 8a, HT 98a, PE 2 | List of parties, each with a fractional quantity and no commodity named |
| C f C f C f | 14 | 0.14 | HT 23a, 35, 50a, 91, **KH 8, KH 15** | List of commodities, each in fractions |
| W N f W N f | 5 | 0.45 | HT 9a, 19, 36, 104 | Parties with mixed quantities |
| \ | W \ | W C N | 5 | 0.19 | HT 28b, 96a, **KH 5, KH 7a** | Two separated words before the commodity |

The format `C f C f C f` is Khania's, appearing in two of its six documents, and anticipates by an independent route what §4 establishes about that archive. A classification error corrected during the test should be declared: the edition writes some values as approximations ("≈ ¹⁄₆") and the classifier did not recognise them as fractions, which generated a spurious motif.

### 1.4 The list entries: groups, modules and metrology

The most numerous class of the corpus, the entries followed by a figure, was also the only one without an instrument. Four tests failed: the profile of endings within the class (one ending shared by two roots, null 0.4, p = 0.36), concentration by site (an artefact: 87% in a single site against 32% expected, but the list is headed by KU-RO and KI-RO, and within Haghia Triada none of the twenty entries concentrates on a scribe, p = 1.00), the stability of quantities (entries vary less than commodities, 0.95 against 1.54, the opposite of what was predicted) and the suffix -JU (11 of 317 against 1 of 344, p = 0.0004, which passes correction but with no bare root to confirm it). The fifth works.

#### 1.4.1 1 The entries recur in groups, and this was seen in 1974

Prior art, checked item by item. Packard [11] (1974, pp. 62-63) first observed that HT 86 and HT 95 form a connected pair. Uchitel and Finkelberg [10] (1995) set out three tables of recurrent items by inspection. Compared against the groups measured here:

- Their Table I is group C exactly, down to the six items *ku-ni-su*, *sa-ru*, *di-de-ru*, *qa-ra₂-wa/qe-ra₂-u*, *da-me* and *mi-nu-te* under the headings *a-ka-ru*, *a-du* and *da-du-ma-ta*. That group is theirs, and the contribution here is only its measurement.
- Their Tables IIa and IIb are not groups A and B. Their IIa holds fifteen items and shares four with group A; their IIb holds ten and shares three with group B, and four more with group A. The two partitions overlap substantially and do not coincide: theirs is organised by document, ours by co-occurrence above a null, and ours separates A from B where theirs distributes members of A across both tables.

Two of their observations were verified directly against the corpus and hold: *ku-ni-su*, an item of group C, does head HT 10a; and *ki-ki-ra-ja*, which they read as a form related to *ki-ro*, does head HT 85b.

Their reading of the items as men from six villages under different headings is stated by inspection, without a null, and is recorded here as prior art rather than as support. The agreement with the conclusion reached below is not evidence: two routes can share a premise about what a list of six with quantities must be.

If the entries are parties of an administration, the same ones should reappear together from one document to another. Over the 56 documents with three or more entries, setting aside the units already read, the pairs coinciding in two or more documents number 35 against 8.2 expected under a null that reassigns entries preserving the size of each document and the frequency of each entry (600 repetitions, maximum of the null 18). p < 0.002. ### 1.5 The aromatics file

#### 1.5.1 The identification of \*303, which is disputed and is measured here

The reading of \*303 as cyperus, which this work follows after the edition consulted, is not unanimous. Goold and Pope (1955), followed by Uchitel [4] (2003), identify it with barley; Palmer (1995) proposed emmer wheat. All three identifications rest on the shape of the sign, on its relative frequency, or on comparison of resources between archives, and none on its quantitative distribution within the corpus. It is worth measuring, because the premise conditions everything that follows.

| Sign | n | Median | Maximum | Records with a fraction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GRA (grain, identified by inheritance from Linear B [13]) | 90 | 15.62 | 976 | 13% |
| Olive | 21 | 10.00 | 93.5 | 29% |
| Wine | 34 | 3.62 | 104 | 29% |
| Oil | 100 | 3.62 | 100 | 26% |
| Figs | 50 | 2.00 | 75 | 34% |
| **\*303** | **64** | **0.62** | **23** | **61%** |

Most records of \*303 are of less than one unit and six in ten carry a fraction, against the grain of the same archive, whose median is twenty-five times larger and which records fractions in one case in eight. Company differs likewise: \*303 does not coincide with the olive in any of its 53 documents, while grain does so in twelve of seventy-two.

The control is in Linear B, where the identification is not disputed: there grain reaches 10,300 units and cyperus ten. The profile of \*303 (median 0.62, maximum 23, mostly fractions) is the second and not the first.

This does not prove \*303 is cyperus. It proves it is not counted as a cereal, which rules out the most cited alternative reading and leaves the field to substances dosed in fractions. It also answers the argument from frequency: \*303 can be the most recorded commodity of Khania without being a grain, if Khania was a centre of aromatics production, which is what the chemistry of the region's workshops indicates.

One datum tells against this reading and must be recorded. On the four tablets that combine a personnel section with a commodity section (HT 27a, HT 89, HT 94a, HT 100), the commodity section always follows the same order, \*303 followed by figs and wine, with additions at the end; it is the sequence Uchitel (2003) calls Standard II. That \*303 systematically heads that sequence places it in the position of the principal product, which is the position a cereal would occupy in a ration list. Position alone does not decide, but it is an argument for the alternative reading and not for the quantitative profile set out above.

#### 1.5.2 Oil divides into two classes

The oil logograms of Linear A divide into two groups by their quantities and their company. OLE+U, plain OLE, OLE+KI, OLE+MI, OLE+DI and OLE+TU are recorded in whole units (medians between 3 and 7, up to 54) and share documents with cereal, olives, figs and \*304, above chance in every case: these are the oils of estate production. OLE+TA, OLE+RI and OLE+NE are recorded in fractions and share documents with \*303 (OLE+TA in 4 of 7 documents, p < 0.001), with \*308, MI+JA+RU and the ligatures QA₂+[?]+PU and QA₂+[?]+RE, and almost never with cereal or olives. The second group corresponds in function to the qualified oils of the Fr series at Pylos (*ku-pa-ro-we*, *wo-do-we*, *pa-ko-we*), with the qualifier written as a bound sign instead of as a word, a device Linear B also uses in CYP+KU and PYC+KU, and which the editors of the new ivory inscriptions from the Knossos cult centre describe in the same terms (Kanta, Nakassis, Palaima and Perna [6] 2024). The values of TA, RI and NE are unknown, and OLE+TA, the one most often recorded with \*303, is the natural candidate for cyperus oil.

#### 1.5.3 The ingredient block

The four signs accompanying the qualified oils form a closed block. Over the 1,719 documents of the corpus, \*308 (8 documents), MI+JA+RU (7), QA₂+[?]+PU (5) and QA₂+[?]+RE (5) co-occur with \*303 and with OLE+TA, OLE+RI and OLE+NE far above expectation (hypergeometric, p < 0.001 in every case), are always recorded in fractions, and \*308 and MI+JA+RU never appear with cereal or figs. A fifth sign the first pass had included, \*188, does not belong to the block: it is recorded in whole units, heads documents and appears with the KI-RO deficits, so it is another commodity and is withdrawn from the file.

A limitation of this test must be declared. The documents containing the block are richer in logograms than the corpus average (4.0 against 1.4), and that richness inflates any co-occurrence: the hypergeometric test controls for how many documents each sign appears in, but not for how many logograms each document carries (capture-artefact control, r = 0.415). What sustains the result is therefore not the raw co-occurrence but two things richness does not explain: that the fixed-margin null, which does preserve document sums, produces the same clustering; and that the contrast between what the block accompanies (oil and olive, 7 documents against 1.9 expected, p = 0.002) and what it never accompanies (textiles, zero of fourteen) is asymmetric, where richness would affect both alike.

The grouping of these documents is not new either. Uchitel (2003) classifies the Haghia Triada tablets into standard commodity sequences, and his Standard Sequence III gathers HT 23a, HT 32, HT 33 and HT 35, four of the five central tablets of this file, on a different criterion. And in the same work he identifies SA-SA-ME as the Minoan equivalent of Mycenaean *sa-sa-ma*, "sesame"; that identification is taken from him and not offered as our own. What this work adds to the grouping is its measurement.

#### 1.5.4 The format: the Minoan spice lists

HT 16 deserves separate mention. Its entries are single signs used as commodity abbreviations followed by small fractions (WA ⅓, \*188+KU ⅜, SA-PO ZE ¹⁄₁₆), a format found among Linear A tablets only in HT 20 as well, written by the same hand according to Godart's attribution (GORILA V [1], scribe HT 10), on which the scope of palaeography as a criterion is discussed by Salgarella and Judson [8], so that the format is in part a scribal habit. It is the format of the Ge series at Mycenae, in which condiments are recorded by acrophonic abbreviation with the smallest measures of the system.

#### 1.5.5 Sesame in this file

SA-SA-ME (AB 31-31-13) is attested once, on HT 23a, with the fraction ¹⁄₁₆, in the same type of document that supports the three previous cases. In the Ge series at Mycenae sesame, *sa-sa-ma*, occupies that same position, that of the smallest measures of the system, beside fennel, safflower and mint. Greek σήσαμον is a recognised Semitic loan (Akkadian *šamaššammū*, Ugaritic *ššmn*), and the Minoan form preserves the reduplication of the source with its own final vowel, which places it as a link in the Minoan channel that Melena and Piquero [7] (2015) postulate for commodity terms.

The case belongs to a different class from the previous three and is declared as such: it is not a name formed on a plant name but the word for the commodity itself. Its force is smaller: a single attestation, no family, no affixes and no possible co-occurrence null; and the ambiguity between commodity and party, which the seals decided in the case of KU-PA, cannot be decided here. What inclines towards the commodity is the format of the document, the absence of derivatives and of any appearance in a header, roundel or personnel list, and the exact parallel of the Ge series.

#### 1.5.6 Discussion of the aromatics file

What is described is an administration of aromatics and perfumed oils: a class of oil measured in fractions and allocated together with cyperus and four fixed ingredients, in documents with their own format, in which the allocation header SA-RA₂ appears twice followed by cyperus and OLE+TA. The Mycenaeans, two centuries later, write the same thing with words: the oil is called "of cyperus", "of rose", "of sage". The Minoans wrote it with ligatures, which is why the file had not been seen: it is not in the vocabulary but in the signs bound to the logograms.

What cannot be decided. First, the values of TA, RI and NE: they are acrophonic qualifiers of something, and the corpus does not say of what; OLE+TA is a candidate for "cyperus oil", a hypothesis and not a reading. Second, the identity of \*308, MI+JA+RU and the two QA₂ ligatures: their distribution places them in the file and nothing more. Third, whether the proportions are a recipe: the ingredients appear with equal quantities within the same document more often than the corpus quantities predict (0.26 against 0.06; p = 0.0005), but not more than a null restricted to fractions predicts (0.21; p = 0.24), so the question is undecidable with the legible quantities.

The Minoan channel, measured over the whole semantic field. The hypothesis that certain commodity terms reached Greek from the East through Minoan has been argued word by word. With the list of twenty Bronze Age perfume substances, closed by the Assur recipes and the Mycenaean series, it can be measured as a whole: 90% of their Greek names are not Indo-European (18 of 20), against 35% of a control set of twenty concrete nouns from other domains (7 of 20; Fisher exact test, p = 0.0008). And within the non-Indo-European ones, the pre-Greek outnumber the Semitic two to one. When the Greeks inherited the perfume industry they inherited its lexicon too, and that lexicon was neither theirs nor, for the most part, directly Eastern. A qualification is due: "pre-Greek" is not "Minoan", since it designates the Aegean substrate in general; what points to Minoan specifically is that six of those substances have an attested counterpart in Linear A itself.

What would refute this work. Three observations would. First, that residue analysis of processing vessels from a Minoan aromatics workshop finds no *Cyperus* markers (cyperene, α-cyperone, cyperotundone, rotundene) while finding those of the other proposed ingredients. Second, that a new tablet records \*303 in large whole quantities beside olives or livestock, which would place it in the profile of a cereal. Third, that the equality of fraction between secondary entries turns out to be an artefact of fraction notation rather than a property of the documents.

### 1.6 The archive of Khania: another format, another practice

Haghia Triada is 64% of the corpus, and everything above describes its archive. Khania, with 227 documents and 105 tablets, is the second usable series, and measured against its own base rates it turns out to be something else.

Khania never sums. None of its 105 tablets carries KU-RO, KI-RO, PO-TO-KU-RO or DA-I; at Haghia Triada 41 of 205 do (20%). At the HT rate, the probability of having none in 105 tablets is 7·10⁻¹¹. Across the whole Khania corpus, one document of 227 carries a summation term (0.4%, against 3.7% at HT). The difference is one of administrative practice, not of sample size.

Khania records something else, and in another way. Its dominant commodity is CYP (65 occurrences against 14 at HT), and 68% of its CYP records carry a fraction, against 36% at HT. Its tablets have 7.4 units on average against 13.8, a quarter of the syllabic groups per tablet (0.8 against 3.1), and smaller figures (median 3 against 5). It is an archive of fine distribution of fine products, against the bulk store of Haghia Triada. §2 3 gives the cause: cyperus is not counted in tens anywhere in the corpus, so an archive dominated by cyperus records in fractions and has less to total.

What Khania does not have: headers of its own (with a base rate of first position of 15% over its 99 tablets of three or more units, only A-DU passes bare, and it is the header already fixed at HT) nor qualifiers of its own.

What Khania does give: CYP+D as a fixed-quantity allocation. At Khania, CYP+D carries the same quantity (½) in 12 of its 16 attestations with a figure, against a base rate of 22% for that figure among all quantified logograms of the archive (binomial p = 10⁻⁵). No commodity of the Haghia Triada archive has a dominant quantity at that level. And it is issued after personnel entries: KH 7a gives VIR+\*313b 10 followed by CYP+D ½; VIR+\*313b 4 followed by CYP+D ⅓. It is one of the two quantities in the corpus that behave like a ration, the other being group C's grain in tens (§2 1), and it would be refuted by new documents giving CYP+D large or varied quantities, or with no persons around.

Consequence for everything above. The functions fixed in Parts 1 to 3 were fixed on the archive of Haghia Triada. KU-RO as a total is a reading of that archive; at Khania there is nothing to check it against, and its absence there is not accidental. The inventory should state, entry by entry, in which archive each function was fixed, and general claims about "the Minoan receipt" hold for Haghia Triada and weaken outside it.

### 1.7 Discussion

What this inventory changes is not the number of readings but their status. Each one carries a statement of what observation would refute it, and the dictionary refuses to file an entry without that line. Each instrument carries its error rate, and a rate that came out wrong is kept as a record of why the null was changed and not the instrument. Each claim with prior art carries the citation and the measurement together, and those that fall are filed as fallen, with the source against.

And what the inventory says about Linear A, read whole, is something a century of proposals had not said in this form: we know what was counted and how the account was organised, we know three places, and we know no word. The distance between an archive that is understood and a language that is read is exactly that, and no internal method closes it.

To that, the parts together add a picture none gives alone. The Minoan receipt is that of its period and region, with no feature separating it from Ebla or Pylos in what it does, which means the problem of Linear A is not in the administration, which is understood, but in the language, which is not. The inventory says how far understanding the administration goes: 151 functions, almost all an accounting archive has. The aromatics file shows that within that administration one can descend to the class of a product without reading a word. The groups, the modules and the metrology show that the administration allocated in quotas and that the quota depended on the commodity. And what remains open is what it always was, said more precisely: the step from function to word, for which this archive contains no judge.

## Part II. The sealing system

The following work bears directly on what is measured here and is credited accordingly: A search of the NESTOR bibliography returns the reference treatments of this document type, which bears directly on what is measured here: Hallager 2021, "The Minoan Roundel, its function, seals and inscriptions"; Weingarten 2017, "When One Equals One: The Minoan Roundel", and Weingarten 2010, "Minoan Seals and Sealings" (Oxford Handbook); Tomas 2012 on the transition from the Linear A to the Linear B sealing system; and Panagiotopoulos 2022 on Minoan nodules. The measurements stand; the attribution does not.

#### Summary

The Minoan archive writes in two systems: the tablets, which count, and the nodules, roundels and sealings, which identify, the distinction is Schoep's [4]. This chapter describes the second with the procedure used for the first: measured distribution against a permutation null. Four results. First, the two systems share almost no vocabulary: of the 443 intact syllabic units, 389 occur only on tablets, 47 only on sealings and seven on both, against 34.4 expected under a null that preserves how often each unit occurs and how many units each document carries. Second, the reason: the sealing system does not write, it marks. Twenty-eight signs mark the 854 single-sign nodules of Haghia Triada, and five of them cover 83%. Third, mark and seal are paired: only 18 of the 42 identified seals serve more than one mark, against 24.4 under the null. Fourth, the marks fall into two circuits with their own dominant seal, and the two circuits are told apart by the iconography of those seals as well: one is 70% human figure and the other 70% animal. An edition artefact that came close to producing a false result is also declared, since it affects any analysis of sequence over this material.

Keywords: Linear A, nodules, seals, CMS, Neopalatial administration, null models.

#### 1.7.1 The two systems share no vocabulary

Of the 443 intact syllabic units of the corpus, 389 occur only on tablets, 47 only on sealing supports, and seven on both: \*409-VS, KA-KU-PA, PA-SE-JA, PA-TA-NE, \*188-DU, KU-MI-NA-QE and SA-RA. A null that distributes the units across documents preserving how often each one occurs and how many each document carries (800 repetitions) expects 34.4 crossings, with a minimum of 22. p < 0.00125.

This is not physical disconnection: the nodules were tied to the goods the tablets counted. It is that they name different things.

The seven that cross share a profile: they are parties whose goods leave a trace in both places. PA-TA-NE is the clean case, a list entry taking the figure 1 among other entries on HT 94b and HT 122a, and a name on nodule HT Wa 1019 of the same archive. PA-SE-JA carries 20 on the tablet and ¹⁄₂ on the sealing; KA-KU-PA opens HT 16 and stands beside cyperus on sealings.

#### 1.7.2 The sealing system marks, it does not write

Of the 854 single-sign nodules of the GORILA edition [1] of Haghia Triada, twenty-eight distinct signs mark them, and five cover 83%: \*301 (225), KU (151), KA (149), SI (92), RO (88). Against the 443 units the accounts use, the sealing system operates with twenty-eight marks. That accounts for the previous result: the intersection is small because the sealing repertoire is small.

#### 1.7.3 Mark and seal are paired

Of the 42 seals identified on the nodules of Haghia Triada, only 18 are used with more than one mark, against 24.4 expected by reassigning the marks at random (400 repetitions, minimum of the null 21). p < 0.0025. Each seal attends to its mark.

#### 1.7.4 Two circuits, told apart by what they depict

| Circuit | Dominant seal | Marks | Impressions |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | AT 125 | KU, KA, SI, RO, I, TA, DA-KA, SI-KA | 455 |
| II | AT 13 | \*301, ZE | 312 |

Circuit I gathers eight marks, four of them among the five most frequent, with AT 125 dominant in every one (from 38% to 100%). Circuit II consists of \*301, the most frequent mark of the archive with 271 sealed nodules, and ZE, both under AT 13.

The iconography separates them equally. The seal motifs are those of the CMS [2]. The dominant motif of circuit I is "man in kilt and man in robe walking left" (246 impressions); of circuit II, "bird flying right" (143). Aggregated by category, circuit I is 70% human figure and 3% animal; circuit II is 70% animal and 14% human. The difference statistic gives 684 against 3.5 under the null (maximum 21 over 500 reassignments), p < 0.002.

Two qualifications are filed with the result. The effect is carried in large part by one seal per circuit, so these are not two broad opposed repertoires but two principal seals of different class with their tail. And the motif classifier works on keywords and has gaps (stags did not fall in the animal list), so the direction of the result is firm but the exact percentages should not be cited as final.

#### 1.7.5 An edition artefact declared

Looking for sealing batches produced striking series: 225 consecutive nodules marked \*301 (HT Wa 1623 to 1848), 151 with KU, 149 with KA. They belong to the catalogue, not to the archive. Of the 854 single-sign nodules, 825 consecutive pairs share a sign where 135 would be expected if they were mixed: GORILA orders them by their mark, a hazard of the kind Packard [5] warned of. Any analysis of sequence, neighbourhood or batch over the nodules inherits that ordering and produces false results unless it declares it. It is set down here because the material is in common use and the artefact is not flagged in the edition.

#### 1.7.6 Discussion

The archive of Haghia Triada sealed with two offices, told apart by their marks, by their seals and by the class of image those seals carry. The description does not depend on reading a single word, and it is the most complete Minoan administrative structure that has been measured.

What distinguishes the two offices in function remains open: whether they divide by commodity, by destination, by period or by rank. The nodule typology of Hallager [3] would decide what distinguishes the two offices in function. The seal data carry their iconographic description but not their findspot within the site, the information that would settle it.

One figure bears on the wider debate about reading Linear A: \*301, the sign that the most recent Semitic proposals read as *na*, is the most frequent nodule mark of the archive, defines a circuit of its own under AT 13, and stands alone in 238 of its 288 attestations. Such a sign may also carry a syllabic value, but whoever assigns one must explain why that value leaves no trace in its dominant use.

## 2. An Egyptian pattern in the libation formula

The following work bears directly on what is measured here and is credited accordingly: A search of the NESTOR bibliography (34,515 entries, 2006-2026) returns five treatments of the libation formula that bear directly on what is measured here and are credited accordingly: Davis 2013 on its word order, Davis 2014 (*Minoan Stone Vessels with Linear A Inscriptions*, Aegaeum 36), a monograph on the whole corpus of inscribed stone vessels; Valério 2007, "Diktaian Master", which reads *(-)du-pu₂-re* as a term of rulership and *j/a-di-ki-te-te-du-pu₂-re* as the name of a Minoan deity preceding Diktaian Zeus; Witczak 2009, "Cretan Toponyms in the Minoan Libation Formula", on precisely the place names of slot 2; and Soldani 2012. Also Aspesi 1996 on *(-)du-pu-re*. The Egyptian comparison of this chapter is unaffected, but the treatment of the formula's slots must be attributed.

### Summary

The votive inscriptions of Linear A share a formula that recurs at six Cretan sanctuaries over more than two centuries and remains unread. We propose three things about it, fixed by its distribution and not by a borrowed language: that its architecture is that of the Egyptian offering formula, and of no other contemporary tradition, measured position by position against 1,357 real instances of that formula; that U-NA-KA-NA-SI is the term for the offering; and that what can be predicted of a new vessel has a ceiling of 14% that belongs to the corpus. Over the eighteen non-accounting inscriptions preserving at least one anchor it establishes that the formula opens with a fixed word whose root is invariant and whose ending varies by sanctuary; that the fillers of the variable slots are hapax in 32 of 32 cases (probability 10⁻⁴), so that the slots hold the open class of names and the anchors the closed class; that the one inscription with logograms, SY Za 2, places the oil logogram immediately after U-NA-KA-NA-SI, which is the only positive evidence in the corpus for what the formula offers, and which rests on a single tokenisation that cannot be checked against a second transliteration. Compared as a structure with the contemporary votive formulae that are read, the Minoan template places its central invariant element between the names and the offering, as the Egyptian does. Two structural readings survive for the central anchor and two for the next; the datum that would separate them is absent from the corpus, and where it might appear is stated. A held-out test with baselines closes the chapter: reconstructing each inscription's slots from the others recovers 8 of 57 elements (14.0%); an n-gram baseline does better at Top-1 (17.5%); both are bounded by the same fact, that the true filler is among the candidates in only 14.0% of slots. All converge near 23%, a ceiling of the corpus and not of any model.

### 2.1 A text that repeats, and that nobody reads

Around 1600 BC, at six mountain and cave sanctuaries of Crete, one and the same statement was cut on stone vessels, libation tables and ladles. It is recognisable at a glance: it opens with A-TA-I-\*301-WA-JA, goes on with a word that changes from vessel to vessel, and continues with JA-SA-SA-RA-ME, U-NA-KA-NA-SI, I-PI-NA-MA and SI-RU-TE, almost always in that order and almost always the same. It is the only text of Linear A that repeats, and therefore the only one with something like a visible grammar. A century after its identification (Evans; Duhoux 1992; Finkelberg 1990-91; Davis 2013, 2014; Karnava 2016; Petrakis and Steele 2025), nobody knows what it says.

What has been tried is to read it with a borrowed language. Finkelberg (1990-91) equated (J)A-SA-SA-RA-ME with Luwian *ishassaras*, "lady", and hence a deity, the most cited proposal; Facchetti and Negri (2003) read the opening as a divine name; others have read the formula from Semitic or from Greek. None of those readings can be compared with the others, because each starts from a different language and none says what observation in the corpus would refute it. For a century the formula has been the place where every hypothesis about Minoan is put to the test, and where none has been able to lose.

This chapter asks the question the other way round: not what the formula says in another language, but what it says by itself. A text that repeats with slots fixes, by its distribution alone, what each position does: which is fixed and which open, which carries names, which carries the offering, which varies with the place. That can be established without reading a word, against a null that says how much of it chance would produce, and it can be compared with the votive formulae of the same period that are read. It can be taken further: a fixed template predicts, and one can measure how much of a new vessel it recovers from the others, and why not more.

Three things result. First, the Minoan formula has the architecture of the Egyptian votive formula, not that of the Semitic or the Greek: a central invariant element between the names and the offering, and a fixed formula before the dedicant. Second, U-NA-KA-NA-SI is the term for the offering, fixed by a logogram and not by an etymology: on the one vessel that carries logograms it is followed by the sign for oil. Third, the template has a prediction ceiling of 14%, and that ceiling belongs not to the method but to the corpus: 72% of the fillers occur on no other vessel, and no procedure, ours or the reference n-gram, recovers what is not there.

With that, of the dozen readings in circulation two remain per position for the two central anchors, and one can say which inscription would be needed to decide between them: a datum that is not in the corpus, and that an excavator would recognise if it appeared. That is what this chapter offers the field: not one more reading, but the exact shape of the one that is missing.

### 2. Data and method

Eighteen non-accounting inscriptions preserve at least one anchor: IO Za 2, 6, 9, 14, 15, 16 (Iouktas); KN Za 10 (Knossos); KO Za 1 (Kophinas); PK Za 11, 12, 27 (Palaikastro); PL Zf 1 (Platanos); PS Za 2 (Psychro); SY Za 2, 3 (Syme); TL Za 1 (Tylissos); VRY Za 1 (Vrysinas); AP Za 2 (Apodoulou). Corpus: GORILA (Godart and Olivier 1976-1985) in the form of the LinearA Explorer, with sign verification in SigLA.

A caveat on the size of the votive corpus. Classifying by support inflates it: of the 107 stone vessels inscribed in Linear A, 33 are accounting documents and only 15 carry the formula, and the 33 cluster at Zakros, with up to twelve quantities on a single piece. The votive corpus of Linear A is seventeen documents, not a hundred and seven, and that sets the statistical power of everything that follows (Acedo 2026b).

The measurements are distributional: the position of each anchor, the hapax rate of the fillers, co-occurrence with logograms, and the variation of the endings cross-tabulated with site. Where a null is needed, it is a permutation of labels within strata.

Linear A sign-groups are read here by applying Linear B values to Linear A signs, the approach recent scholarship regards as methodologically legitimate for the study of Linear A (Steele and Meißner 2017), and are given in capitals with hyphens, the convention of Younger (2024) and of Salgarella and Petrakis (2025). ### 2.2 The template, fixed by distribution

#### 2.2.1 The opening is a root with a paradigm
A note on notation first. The transliteration used splits \*301 off as a separate unit, which conceals the opening anchor; here it is restored to its word. The sign \*301 is left without a phonetic value, following GORILA and the edition consulted. Part of the decipherment literature assigns it the value JO and therefore writes A-TA-I-JO-WA-JA where this chapter reads A-TA-I-\*301-WA-JA. The difference is not one of reading the tablets but of assigning a value to a sign, and it affects equally all sixteen inscriptions in which the opening occurs. Nothing that follows depends on that assignment, since \*301 is constant across all variants and what is measured is the variation of the endings. So restored, the opening (A) occurs in sixteen inscriptions and always in first position, with an invariant root (A-)TA-I-\*301 and six endings: -WA-JA (eleven times), -WA-E, -U-JA, -U-TI-NU, -TI and -DE-KA.

The endings group by sanctuary: Syme five of five with -WA-JA; Iouktas three with -WA-JA and one with TA-NA-I-\*301-U-TI-NU; Palaikastro one -WA-E and one -WA-JA; Apodoulou and Psychro with forms of their own. Nor do they covary with the ending of the toponym that follows (-WA-JA occurs with -tu, -TE, -a and -mi), so the variation is one of local convention and not of grammatical agreement (p from 0.011 to 0.16 once site is controlled).

This is the corpus's best candidate for an inflectional or derivational paradigm on a single root, and it is stated as such without proposing what distinguishes each ending. The internal structure of this opening was analysed by Finkelberg (1990-91), and priority belongs to her: she observes that while the beginning and the end of the formula vary, its middle, the sequence -i-\*301-, is stable, and concludes that it cannot be a single word but two semantic units, the first ending in -i, which she proposes to read as an element of nominal or verbal inflection. She further shows that the three endings are neither forms of one word (the element u/wa is absent from -TI, the element ti from -WA-JA) nor three distinct words, and describes them as conglomerates of elements. What is added here is the measurement of what the variation depends on: the endings group by sanctuary and do not covary with the ending of the toponym that follows, so that the variation is one of local convention and not of grammatical agreement.

#### 2.2.2 The slots hold the open class
The slot preceding the central anchor has ten fillers at six sites, and holds toponyms identifiable through Linear B (JA-DI-KI-TU and A-DI-KI-TE = Dikte; I-DA-A and I-DA-MI = Ida) sometimes followed by two further words. The following slots have fourteen fillers.

All thirty-two fillers are hapax: they occur once in the whole corpus. Under the corpus hapax rate (0.76), the probability of thirty-two hapax in a row is of the order of 10⁻⁴. The conclusion is structural and depends on no reading: the variable slots hold names and places, and the anchors the closed class.

#### 2.2.3 SY Za 2 fixes the term for the offering

A single inscription of the series carries logograms, and it decides one position. SY Za 2 writes "JA-SU-MA-TU OLIV | U-NA-KA-NA-SI OLE": anchor D is followed by the logogram for oil, and the word preceding it carries that for olives.

That fixes D as the term for the offering, with no recourse to etymology. It is, so far as I can tell, the only point of the formula whose function is established by internal evidence rather than by comparison with a candidate language. The observation about this inscription is due to Petrakis and Steele (2025); what is added here is its distributional consequence.

#### 2.2.4 The weight that SY Za 2 can carry, and what it cannot

The reading of §3.3 deserves its own limitation, because the whole of it rests on one token. In the edition consulted, SY Za 2 is transliterated with the sign group and the logogram joined, U-NA-KA-NA-SI-OLE. The hyphen there is the convention that joins adjacent elements, and it is demonstrably unreliable elsewhere: on HT Wc 3017 the same convention produces CYP+D-\*304+PA where HT Wc 3016 and HT We/Wc 3020 give \*304+PA-CYP+D, and a lexical sequence does not reverse. SY Za 2 is absent from SigLA, so the segmentation cannot be checked against a second hand.

Two further facts bear on it. Across its nine attestations the sign group varies in form (U-NA-RU-KA-NA-SI on IO Za 16, U-NA-RU-KA-NA-TI on PK Za 11, U-NA-RU-KA-JA-SI on PK Za 12, JA-SA-U-NA-KA-NA-SI on PK Za 8), which is not how a fixed commodity term behaves; and a reading of the position as a body of worshippers rather than as the thing offered is compatible with every other datum in the corpus, and has been proposed (di Mino 2026). What the corpus fixes is therefore the position, not the sense: SY Za 2 shows that this is where the formula names something that can be measured in oil. What decides between the two readings is the page of GORILA IV, and it is named here so that whoever holds the volume can settle it.

#### 2.2.5 The limits on JA-SA-SA-RA-ME
The central anchor occurs thirteen times at six sanctuaries, with variants of prefix (ja-, a-, none) and of ending (-ME, -MA, -MA-NA), and never carries a logogram or a quantity. That absence, across thirteen occurrences and with SY Za 2 showing that the formula does admit logograms, rules out two functions: JA-SA-SA-RA-ME designates neither the offering nor the object. And its invariance across sanctuaries rules out two more: it is not the dedicant's name (which would be hapax) nor the sanctuary's (which would vary by site). This agrees with the conclusion of Petrakis and Steele (2025), who find that the recent evidence does not support the reading of (J)A-SA-SA-RA-ME as a theonym.

#### 2.2.6 The resulting template
[A: fixed root + local ending] [place, and up to two names: hapax] [C: JA-SA-SA-RA-ME, no logogram] [D: U-NA-KA-NA-SI, admits a logogram of offering] [E: I-PI-NA-MA] [F: SI-RU-TE] [name or second form of the opening: hapax, optional]

With a variant Younger called "abbreviated", at Syme: opening, word with OLIV, D with OLE, and logograms in place of anchors E and F.

#### 2.2.7 Separable morphology

Three elements separate by distribution and not by etymology.

I-NA-: two root/prefixed pairs in the whole corpus, both in formula context and both on a place name: I-NA-I-DA against I-DA (Ida), and I-NA-JA-PA-QA against JA-PA-QA, these two at the same sanctuary (PK Za 11 and 12). A candidate for a marker of place: located, not read. Younger discusses the prefix I- as a case marker; I-NA- is a longer form with the same distribution.

ja- / a-: eleven pairs with a- in the whole corpus (A-RI-JA / RI-JA, A-KI-RO / KI-RO, A-MI-TA / MI-TA), and the alternation JA-SA-SA-RA-ME / A-SA-SA-RA-ME / SA-SA-RA-ME. Its frequency is 7 per thousand at Haghia Triada against 140-240 per thousand at the sanctuaries: it is a feature of genre, not of grammar.

U-TI-NU: occurs only on IO Za 11, and as the ending of TA-NA-I-\*301-U-TI-NU (IO Za 6) and of TA-NA-RA-TE-U-TI-NU (IO Za 2). The two Iouktas compounds share the frame TA-NA-[X]-U-TI-NU. It is a separable element that the script joins to the preceding word without a divider, and it has a consequence for the template: the tail filler of IO Za 2 is not a name but a second form of the opening, so that at Iouktas the formula closes as it opens.

### 2.3 The Egyptian pattern

The template of §3 can be compared, as a structure and without reading a word of it, with the votive formulae of the same period that are read. Four traditions are relevant by contact and date: the Egyptian offering formula, the Anatolian dedications in Hittite and Hieroglyphic Luwian, the Semitic dedications of Ugarit and Phoenicia, and, later, the Greek dedication. Each has a fixed order of its constituents (Table 1).

Table 1. Constituent order of four votive traditions against the Minoan template.

| Tradition | Order | Shares with Minoan |
|---|---|---|
| Egyptian, *ḥtp-dj-nsw* (Barta 1968; Gardiner 1957, 170-173) | [fixed opening formula] [deity] [offerings] [beneficiary] | Fixed opening before the dedicant; invariant element before the offering |
| Anatolian, Hittite votive texts and Luwian dedications (de Roos 2007; Hawkins 2000) | [dedicant and title] [object] [verb] [deity in the dative], or [deity] [dedicant] [verb] | Dedicant before the invariant element and the offering |
| Semitic, Ugaritic and Phoenician dedications (Pardee 2002) | [dedicant] [verb] [object] [to the deity] | None: the deity closes the text |
| Greek, archaic dedications (Lazzarini 1976) | [dedicant] [verb] [deity in the dative] | None: the deity closes the text |

The Minoan template has two structural traits. The first is the order *names → invariant element (C) → offering (D)*: the dedicant and place come first, the fixed central element next, the offering after it. That order is shared with the Egyptian and the Anatolian formulae and excludes the Semitic and the Greek, which close with the deity. The second is the fixed opening before the dedicant: A-TA-I-\*301-WA-JA stands before any name in every complete inscription (Figure 2). Among the four traditions only the Egyptian opens so: *ḥtp-dj-nsw*, "an offering which the king gives", precedes the deity and the beneficiary in every instance of the formula from the Old Kingdom onward (Barta 1968). Hittite votive texts open with the dedicant or with the deity (de Roos 2007); Ugaritic and Greek dedications open with the dedicant.

The only tradition that matches both traits is the Egyptian; the Anatolian matches one. That is what the title claims, and it is stated with its measure: two traits of two against one of two, on eighteen fragments, with the fixed opening as the decisive trait. The claim is about the architecture of the formula, not about its language, and it is compatible with the phonotactic result that Minoan resembles none of thirteen candidate languages (Acedo 2026a).

The comparison can be made position by position, and not only as a description. The offering formula is extracted from the AES corpus (Schweitzer 2021, from the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae): 1,357 instances of *ḥtp-ḏj-nswt* with their constituents tagged by that corpus (divine names, epithets and titles, offering substantives). For each instance the relative position of each constituent is computed (0 at the start of the formula, 1 at its end), and the same is done for the eighteen Minoan inscriptions.

Table 2. Relative position of each constituent, Minoan and Egyptian.

| Minoan element | Relative position | Egyptian constituent | Relative position |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, A-TA-I-\*301-WA-JA | 0.04 | The formula itself | 0.00 |
| C, JA-SA-SA-RA-ME | 0.41 | Offering | 0.41 |
| Fillers (names) | 0.52 |, |, |
| D, U-NA-KA-NA-SI | 0.75 | Deity | 0.62 |

The skeleton is the same: a fixed opening at the very start in both, invariant elements in the middle third, closure in the last third. The content of the positions is not. In the Egyptian formula the offering precedes the deity (0.41 against 0.62); in the Minoan template the term for the offering is D, fixed by the logogram that follows it on SY Za 2, and it stands where the Egyptian puts the deity. The coincidence is therefore of architecture and not of constituent order, and the chapter claims no more than that. One consequence is worth stating: a reading of C as a deity, which is what the Luwian equation proposes, gains no support from the Egyptian parallel, since in the Egyptian formula that position belongs to the offering and in the Minoan the offering is fixed one position later.

Three cautions belong here. First, on precedent: the formula has been compared before, but always through a candidate language or a text read in one, with Linear B and Indo-European dedications (Facchetti, Negri 2003), with Hurrian and Anatolian material, and with the Orphic hymns on the basis of SY Za 2; Davis (2013) studied its word order to infer the type of language behind it. A comparison of its architecture, as a structure and without reading it, with the Egyptian offering formula I have not found in that literature nor in the bibliography of Karnava (2016). If a reviewer knows one, it belongs here. Second, on function: *ḥtp-dj-nsw* is a funerary formula, and the Minoan text is a sanctuary dedication. What is compared is the architecture of offering texts, not their use; a shared architecture across different uses is, if anything, the more notable. Third, on interpretation: the most economical account of a shared architecture is contact, and the contact is documented for these very centuries by the Keftiu of the Theban tombs, the Kom el-Hetan list and the Minoan paintings at Avaris. That an administration in Crete adopted the shape of an Egyptian formula for its dedications is what such contact would produce, without adopting the language; the phonotactic result cited above says the language was not adopted. This is offered as the reading of the result, not as part of it.

Two things follow, and the second matters more. The skeleton is the same, measured and not described: a fixed formula opens both traditions at position zero, the invariant elements sit in the middle third, and the closing elements in the last. But the content of the positions is not the same. In the Egyptian formula the offering precedes the deity (0.41 against 0.62). If positional correspondence were read as identification, the Minoan offering would be C and the Minoan deity would be D. The corpus says the opposite: D is the term for the offering, because SY Za 2 follows it with the oil logogram, and C never carries a logogram in thirteen occurrences.

The claim of this section is therefore about the skeleton and not about the content: the Minoan formula has the architecture of the Egyptian one and places its offering where the Egyptian places its deity. That is a stronger statement than a shared outline, and it bears on the readings. A reading of C as a deity, which is what the Luwian equation proposes, cannot draw support from the Egyptian parallel, because in the Egyptian formula that position is the offering's and in Minoan the offering is fixed one slot later.

The result has a consequence for the most cited reading. The equation of (j)A-SA-SA-RA-ME with Luwian *ishassaras*, "lady", places a deity in slot C. Under the Anatolian order that is where the deity may stand; under the Egyptian order the deity stands after the fixed opening and before the offering, which is also C. The reading therefore survives the order test in both, but it loses the second trait: no Anatolian formula opens with a fixed element before the dedicant, and the Minoan does. The Luwian reading is not refuted by the architecture; it is deprived of the structural support it has been given, since the architecture points elsewhere.

### 2.4 What the template predicts, and what nothing can predict

Everything above describes the corpus. This section tests prediction on material that was set aside, following
the evaluation practice of textual restoration (Sommerschield et al. 2023): a held-out test, several
baselines including the field's standard n-gram, and accuracy reported at several ranks rather than
at the first alone.

Design. The eighteen inscriptions in which the opening anchor can be located are indexed by
position relative to that anchor. For each slot, the whole inscription containing it is withheld and
the element is predicted from the seventeen others by what occupies that relative position, weighting
a candidate by how many other elements its source inscription shares with the one being reconstructed.
The anchor position is excluded from scoring, since it is what the alignment uses; an early run
that did not exclude it scored 20.2% and owed eleven of its seventeen hits to that position alone.

Results, with baselines and at several ranks:

|  | Top-1 | Top-3 | Top-5 | Top-10 | Top-20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| This predictor | *\*14.0%** | 14.0% | 14.0% | 14.0% | 14.0% |
| N-gram baseline (predict from the preceding element) | *\*17.5%** | 19.3% | 21.1% | 22.8% | 22.8% |

Two things must be said plainly, and the second explains the first.

The n-gram baseline beats this predictor at Top-1, 17.5% against 14.0%. The standard baseline of
the field is better than the method proposed here, and reporting otherwise would require omitting it.

And this predictor's accuracy does not improve with rank at all, which is not how a predictor
behaves unless it has run out of candidates. It has.

|---|---|
|---|---|
| Slots evaluated | 57 |
| The true element is available among the candidates for that position | 8 (*\*14.0%**) |
| The true element occurs in **no other inscription of the formula** | 41 (*\*71.9%**) |

The ceiling of this method is 14.0%, and its accuracy is 14.0%. It recovers every slot the
structure allows it to recover. In the remaining 86% the true element is not available to be
proposed, and in nearly three quarters of cases because it is a hapax.

That is the result, and it confirms section 3.2 from the other side. The slots hold the open
class: all thirty-two of the formula's fillers are hapax. One cannot predict the filler because the
filler is new each time; one can predict the template because the template recurs. The eight hits are
JA-SA-SA-RA-ME, I-PI-NA-MA and SI-RU-TE, which are exactly the three elements section 3 identifies as
fixed by distribution. The same conclusion is reached twice by independent routes.

One figure is worth stating for anyone who attempts this corpus with a stronger method. All three
approaches converge near 23% as rank grows, which is approximately the share of slots occupied by
elements that occur more than once. The ceiling is a property of the corpus, not of the models: no
method that works by recognising repetition will exceed it, however sophisticated, because 72% of the
material to be recovered occurs exactly once.

What would refute this. New formula inscriptions raising the share of repeated fillers, which
would lift the ceiling and make the comparison between methods meaningful; or the hits ceasing to
concentrate on the fixed positions, which would sever the connection with section 3.

Calibration. The instrument was measured on accounting documents where no template exists: it
reports a false positive in 5.0% of 120 trials at the 5% threshold and 0.83% at 1%. The permutation
null for this test, shuffling elements within each inscription, gives 3.5%.

### 2.5 The two readings that survive, and the datum that would separate them

For each position, the functions a votive formula can assign to it are enumerated, drawing on the formulae of Egypt, Anatolia, Ugarit and Greece that are read, and those that contradict a measured datum are discarded.

#### 2.5.1 The central anchor (C)

| Hypothesis | Predicts | Does it hold? |
|---|---|---|
| **C1: name or title of the receiving deity** | Invariant across sites; no logogram; after the names and before the offering | **yes throughout** |
| **C2: verb of dedicating** | Invariant; after the dedicant; might take an object after it | **compatible**, but then D would be the object and C-D would be verb plus object |
| C3: word for offering or object | Should admit a logogram at some point | No: 0 of 13. Discarded |
| C4: dedicant's name | Should be hapax | No: 13 near-identical occurrences. Discarded |
| C5: sanctuary's name | Should vary by site | No: the same form at five sanctuaries. Discarded |

The datum that would separate C1 from C2: if C ever appeared without D and with a logogram of offering after it, it would be a verb with an object; if it appeared in a non-votive text with a name before it and nothing after, it would be the deity. Neither occurs in the present corpus. The place where it might is the Knossos sceptre, a ritual text without numerals.

#### 2.5.2 The offering anchor (D)

| Hypothesis | Predicts | Does it hold? |
|---|---|---|
| **D1: term for the offering** | Admits a logogram; follows the deity | **yes**: OLE on SY Za 2; always after C |
| **D2: verb of giving or offering** | Follows the recipient in the Egyptian and Anatolian formulae; may take an object | **yes as well** |
| D3: dedicant's title | Should vary or be hapax | No: 9 near-identical at 5 sites. Discarded |
| D4: case marker or postposition | Would not take a logogram of its own | No: OLE follows it. Discarded |

D1 and D2 are not exclusive: a verb "to libate" and a noun "libation" share a root in many languages. The alternation -SI / -TI / -JA-SI, if it is morphology, favours D2; if it is local orthography, it decides nothing.

#### 2.5.3 The closings (E and F)
Both are invariable, come last and occur at six sanctuaries. Neither ever carries a logogram, which rules out their being a second object of offering; their invariance rules out their being names. Two functions remain compatible and cannot be separated with this corpus: a fixed closing formula, or an adverb of time or place. One datum is informative: F can occur without either C or D (VRY Za 1, IO Za 15), so the closing depends neither on the deity nor on the offering, but on the inscription itself.

#### 2.5.4 A claim made while this chapter was being written

In June 2026 Tom Di Mino, an engineer without formal training in ancient languages, announced a decipherment of Linear A as an extinct Semitic language, and the pivot of his argument is the formula treated here. He proposes the value *na* for \*301, the sign left without a phonetic value above, which yields in A-TA-I-\*301-WA-JA the Semitic root n-w-y, *nawaya*, "to dwell", attested in Hebrew and Akkadian; from there he reports 42 sign readings, a lexicon of 508 entries and 443 translations (*Ya Diktu: Grammar of the Minoan Peak Sanctuary Libation Formula*, August 2026), built with scripts over GORILA and SigLA. The work is under review at Rutgers and at Cambridge; the method behind the individual assignments has not been published and no second researcher has reproduced them. A Semitic reading of Linear A was proposed by Cyrus Gordon in 1957 and was not accepted.

Two measurements from the companion study bear on it, and neither settles it. First, Ugaritic is among thirteen candidate languages compared with Minoan on consonant-skeleton phonotactics, and its distance to the corpus is indistinguishable from the distance to a Minoan corpus with its syllables shuffled. Second, and closer to the point, a search over the sound values of the unanchored signs, \*301 among them, optimised by simulated annealing against a control corpus, brings no Semitic candidate below its control; the largest z of the whole table is 1.7 and belongs to Luwian. Under no assignment of values compatible with the place names shared with Linear B does the corpus resemble a Semitic language more than a corpus with no language in it resembles one.

That is a statement about the corpus, not about the formula. A votive text can carry loanwords from a language the corpus does not belong to, which is what the trade vocabulary shared with Linear B shows, and a correct reading of one text does not oblige the rest of the corpus to look like the language of that reading. The two results are offered as what they are: the formula is the most measured object in the corpus, and any reading of it now has figures to be consistent with.

### 2.6 Discussion

What is established. The formula has a template of seven positions whose function is fixed by distribution in five of them; its slots hold the open class with a probability of error of the order of 10⁻⁴; its opening is a root with a paradigm whose variation is local and not grammatical; three morphological elements separate by distribution; and one position, that of the offering, is fixed by internal evidence of the corpus itself.

What is not established, and will not be with this corpus: the value of any of the four anchors. The possible functions of each have been enumerated, those contradicting some datum discarded, and two remain per position in the two central cases. The datum that would separate each pair is specified and does not exist: new inscriptions are needed, and which ones would serve is stated.

What this contributes to the debate on the language. Nothing directly, and that should be said. The comparison of templates is one of structure and not of words, so it favours no linguistic affiliation: a community can adopt the ritual structure of its neighbours while speaking another language, as indeed happens throughout the ancient Mediterranean. What it does provide is a criterion for ordering the existing proposals: a reading proposal that requires a Semitic or Greek template has the structure measured here against it, and one that requires the Anatolian or the Egyptian does not. The criterion decides nothing, but it discards.

And what it contributes to method. In a corpus without a bilingual, the function of each position of a formula is measurable and the number of possible readings can be bounded. What is not measurable is the value of the words. That distinction is what this chapter seeks to establish, because a century of proposals has treated the two as one task.

# Part Two. The attempt to read it

*What happens when one tries. Thirteen candidate languages measured against one null, and a proposal of our own that reaches p = 0.0515 and is presented as what that figure allows: candidates, not identifications.*

## 3. Thirteen candidate languages, measured

### Summary

For a century, proposals have assigned Linear A to a language family, Semitic, Luwian, Hittite, Hurrian, Indo-European, and each has exhibited lexical matches in support. None has measured how many matches chance produces. This chapter supplies the missing measurement, and the result is not a ranking of candidates but a verdict on the criterion. Five findings. First, under an anchored search over sign values calibrated against a control corpus, no candidate exceeds z = 2.3, and that one is Luwian, the proposal the field abandoned sixty years ago on archaeological grounds; the Semitic candidates sit at chance (Ugaritic 0.1, Akkadian 0.4) and Hurrian and Etruscan fall below the control (−2.3 and −3.2). Second, under the phonological conversion rules that a CV syllabary imposes and that recent proposals declare explicitly, 89.1% of Linear A units find a Semitic comparandum, Mycenaean Greek finds one 79.7% of the time, and Packard's fictitious grids [1] reach 97.1%: finding a comparandum carries no information. Third, simulated annealing over the 38 free signs finds grids scoring 183 against 75 for the standard Linear B grid [9], changing 37 of 38 signs: optimising for lexical fit actively prefers grids that are almost certainly wrong. Fourth, a screening that requires both the vocalised form and the meaning a measured function demands returns zero candidates over 44,855 glossed entries of Akkadian, Ugaritic and Sumerian, under both a permissive and a strict phonology. Fifth, searching with the functional constraint as the sole objective satisfies 1 of 20 constraints against 0 for the standard grid: with lexical fit alone there are too many solutions and the best are false, with the functional constraint added there are none. Sixth, the phonological profile, measured here for the first time against a null, yields the one hard constraint this work produces, Minoan barely uses the vowel o, 4.7% against 26.7% in Mycenaean, but three structural features give three different answers as to which candidate it favours, which is the result reported for structural phylogenetics generally. Seventh, an information count explains all of the above: specifying a decipherment costs 587 bits and the corpus supplies at most 236, so of the order of 2³⁵¹ assignments fit it equally well. Linear A is not thereby undecipherable but that this corpus cannot decide, and that any proposal resting on the number of translations achieved is using a measure that rewards the opposite of what it seeks.

Keywords: Linear A, decipherment, null models, Packard test, information theory, language classification.

### 3.1 A century of proposals and no common test

The proposals are well known and mutually exclusive. Gordon [2] (1957, 1966) read Minoan as Northwest Semitic; Palmer [3] (1958-61) as Luwian; Davis (1960s) as Hittite; Best (1970s-80s) as Semitic again; Owens (2004) as Indo-European; van Soesbergen [5] (2022) as Hurrian across eight volumes and 4,100 pages; and in August 2026 a new Semitic reading [14] appeared as a preprint with a DOI and an auditable catalogue of readings.

Quantitative work on the corpus exists and is careful where it stays within the archive: the values of the fraction signs were fixed by Corazza and colleagues [6], and Tamburini [7] has surveyed the computational methods applied to undeciphered scripts. What has not been done is the control.

Each exhibits matches. None reports how many matches an incorrect assignment would produce. That control was invented for this very corpus: Packard [1] (1974) built nine fictitious decipherments by redistributing the Linear B values so that no sign kept its own, and compared their matches with those of the correct assignment. No proposal in the list above includes it.

This chapter applies it, and three further tests that follow from it.

The first pass of this search was worthless for reasons that are the reasons this chapter exists: it used five controls, its Minoan skeletons carried editorial marks the control could not reproduce, and its eleven targets ranged from a deduplicated lemma list to raw token dumps of a different kind of object. Corrected, the instrument returns what a calibrated instrument returns when there is nothing to find. Finkelberg [11] (2001) builds a morphological profile of fourteen features and compares it against five Anatolian languages, having first dismantled the Semitic and Luwian proposals for the same methodological failure this chapter measures. Her features rest for the most part on the votive formula rather than on the archive, so her profile and the functional inventory used here describe different genres and do not meet except at *a-du*, where the distributional reading holds (12 of 17 attestations of her verbal paradigm occupy first position, base rate 0.405, p = 0.012). The Semitic candidates are indistinguishable from a corpus with no language. Hurrian and Etruscan sit *below* the control: the real corpus resembles them less than a shuffled corpus does.

### 3.3 Finding a comparandum carries no information

A CV syllabary cannot write voicing or emphasis; sibilants merge into one series; laryngeals often go unwritten; neither consonantal nor vocalic length is marked. Recent proposals declare these rules explicitly, which is a merit and makes them testable. Under them, a Linear A sign group corresponds not to a word but to a *class* of words, and the size of that class is measurable.

Reducing the 615 intact syllabic units to their skeleton under those rules, and the complete Ugaritic lexicon plus 60,000 Akkadian forms likewise:

| Mycenaean, which is Greek | 79.7% |
| **Linear A with a fictitious grid (Packard)** | **97.1%** (min 91.0, max 99.5, 40 grids) |

z of the real corpus against the fictitious grids: −4.71. A deliberately false assignment finds Semitic comparanda *better* than the correct one, and Greek finds them four times in five. This does not refute any reading: it fixes where the weight of a reading must lie, which is not in the existence of a comparandum.

The same holds across languages. Under permissive phonology, the fraction of units finding a match runs from 53.7% (Palaic) to 88.6% (Hurrian), mean 73.8%. The language that matches best is the one that scores worst under the anchored search.

### 3.4 Optimising for lexical fit prefers false grids

Scoring one grid is one thing; searching for the best is another, and no proposal has done it. Simulated annealing over the 38 free signs, with the objective any decipherment pursues, how many units become real words of the target language, plus ten times how many of those also mean what a measured function requires:

|  | Permissive phonology | Strict phonology |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear B grid** | Lexical **75**, functional 0 | Lexical **40**, functional 0 |
| Best found by search | **183** | **131** |
| Signs changed from Linear B | **37 of 38** | **38 of 38** |

Three runs from independent seeds converge. The search finds grids 2.4 to 3.3 times better than the standard grid, and to reach them it changes every free sign. This is Packard's 1974 result redone with a modern optimiser and a real lexicon, and it is stronger than his: fictitious grids do not merely tie, they win.

The criterion is therefore not weak but counterproductive. Any proposal resting on the number of translations achieved is using a measure that rewards the opposite of what it seeks.

### 3.5 Requiring form and meaning together returns nothing

The one criterion that survives optimisation is the functional one, because it is the only one no grid satisfies. It can be applied where an independent inventory fixes what a unit does in the account [8].

For each Linear A unit with a measured function, the glossed lexicons of ORACC and the Copenhagen Ugaritic Corpus [12] (44,855 entries: 28,187 Akkadian across all dialects, 2,297 Ugaritic lemmas with DULAT glosses over 278 KTU tablets, 5,599 Sumerian) were searched for words that both sound like the unit under the grid and mean what its function demands.

| Phonology | Units tested | With a candidate | With an **exact** match |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permissive | 15 | 1 (KU-RO against Sumerian *gu* "entirety", by prefix) | **0** |
| Strict | 15 | **0** | **0** |

Both phonologies were run and both are reported, because the severity of the phonology is not a free choice but a hypothesis about the script, and 243 bits of discrimination ride on it. The result is reliable to the assumption that weighs most.

A methodological note. With consonantal skeletons alone, four candidates appeared, two of them attractive: KU-RO and KI-RO both matched Akkadian *kalû* "all" with the meanings the distribution requires, total and deficit. They are spurious. KU-RO and KI-RO share a consonantal skeleton and have distinct meanings fixed by distribution, so they cannot be the same word. Linear A writes its vowels, and discarding them is discarding information the corpus has. No screening that fails to distinguish KU-RO from KI-RO is worth running, and none published so far makes that distinction.

### 3.6 And no grid satisfies them either

The screening above ran on one grid, the standard Linear B values. That leaves the obvious defence open: perhaps the values are wrong rather than the language. The defence can be tested, not by enumerating the space, which is impossible, but by searching it with the functional constraint as the sole objective.

Twenty units whose measured function fixes a semantic class (five headers, three totals, one deficit, one offering, three place names, five parties, two textiles) are touched by thirty signs, seventeen of them free. The lexicon is the glossed entries of ORACC and the Copenhagen Ugaritic Corpus sorted by class: 45 words meaning totality, 35 deficit, 52 record, 127 offering, 275 person, 140 place, 152 textile. Objective: how many of the twenty become a word of their class. Simulated annealing over the seventeen free signs, eight independent seeds, 6,000 steps each.

|  | Functions satisfied of 20 |
|---|---|
| The Linear B grid | **0** |
| Best found by annealing | **1**, and it is spurious |

No assignment of values in the space searched makes two of the measured functions come out at once as words of Akkadian, Ugaritic or Sumerian meaning what their position requires.

Set beside §4, that completes a figure worth stating plainly. With lexical fit alone as the criterion there are far too many solutions and the best of them are certainly false; with the functional constraint added there are none. The criterion the field uses yields too many answers, and the criterion that measures what the archive does yields no answer at all. Between the two there is no reading.

Three limits are declared with the result. The lexicon per class is small, between 35 and 275 words, where the real vocabulary of each language is larger and the English glosses do not cover every synonym. Three languages of the thirteen candidates, the only ones with a glossed lexicon available. And seventeen signs, not ninety-four, because only those touch units with a measured function.

### 3.7 The limits of the result

There is a premise under all of the above that this result brings into view and does not settle. We have assumed that a unit whose function is fixed is a word meaning that function: that KU-RO, which sums, is the word for "total". It need not be. It could be an abbreviation, as Linear B writes its commodities; a logogram spelled out; a technical term inherited from another administration; or a name. If the Minoan archive writes its functional vocabulary the way Linear B writes *ku-pa-ro* as CYP+KU, then no language would return those units as words, and the negative above would be a fact about Minoan orthography rather than about Minoan language.

That possibility is opened by this measurement and closed by none. It is the first alternative a reader should raise, and we have no test for it.

### 3.8 Three morphological tests, and a retraction

The recent Semitic proposal rests on three morphological features rather than on the lexicon alone. All three are measurable without any etymology, and all three can be calibrated on languages whose answer is known.

Prefix conjugation. Stems appearing with two or more distinct first units, and how many of those alternations use the Semitic prefix set. The measure calibrates: Akkadian 1.28 and Ugaritic 1.20 against Etruscan 1.08, Mycenaean 0.99 and Hittite 0.89. Linear A scores 0.85, the lowest of the six, but with nine observed against 10.6 expected the interval runs from 0.54 to 1.16. No power: 72 alternating stems do not decide.

The tG stem. An infixed -t- after the first root consonant predicts pairs of units differing by an inserted T-series syllable in second position. Six such pairs exist, and they are the ones one would wish to see: A-TA-DE ~ A-DE, DA-TA-RE ~ DA-RE, I-TA-JA ~ I-JA, A-TI-RU ~ A-RU. Against a shuffled null, p = 0.033. But the same test on the other consonant series gives 6 pairs for R and 6 for S; the T series does not stand out, and with twelve series tested the corrected p is 0.40. Measured, and no signal.

Mimation, and its retraction. Word-final m-syllables occur in 7.1% of Linear A units against 3.1% in Mycenaean, p = 1.5·10⁻⁷, the only measurement in this work that favoured the Semitic hypothesis. A second syllabic control annuls it. The Cypriot syllabary writing Greek gives 6.2% over 693 words. Two syllabaries writing languages without mimation give 3.1% and 6.2%: the rate is set by the orthographic convention for final consonants, not by the language, and Linear A at 7.1% is indistinguishable from the Cypriot rate.

This retraction carries a general lesson worth more than the result: when one script is compared with another, the control must be a script of the same kind, and more than one. A single witness produced a p of 1.5·10⁻⁷ that meant nothing.

### 3.9 The phonological profile, and why three features give three answers

The tests above all concern the lexicon. A second criterion has been available to the field since Lejeune [10] (1956) and has never been measured: the phonological profile of Minoan, and its comparison with the candidates. It is measured here, and it fails in a different way from the lexicon, which is what makes it worth reporting.

#### 3.9.1 1 The o-series, and what it excludes

A caution on the o-series. The scarcity of o-series signs in Linear A is measured here and is not in dispute. What it means is. Four readings stand in the literature and none is ours: that Minoan lacked /o/, in a three-vowel system (the traditional reading, treated by Packard 1974 and by Palaima and Sikkenga 1999); that Minoan had *more* than five vowels (Duhoux 1989, 72-73); that e and o are secondary developments from an original three-vowel system by contraction of i-diphthongs (Davis 2014, 240-241); and, most recently, that the scarcity is due to chances of attestation given how few o-series correspondences are known at all (Meissner and Steele, a position Judson calls persuasive).

Two measurements of our own bear on it, and the second corrects the first. Screened against PHOIBLE, which holds living languages, 112 of 3,020 inventories have a, e, i and u and lack /o/, 7.8% of those with all four, and none is in the eastern Mediterranean. That suggested a Minoan without /o/ would be typologically exceptional.

Screened against BDPROTO, which holds 212 ancient and reconstructed languages, the conclusion reverses: 21% of ancient languages with three or more vowels lack a vowel of o quality, nearly three times the living rate, and seven are of the Near East and Aegean, Hittite (a, e, i, u), Akkadian (a, e, i, u), Palaic, Cuneiform Luwian, Urartian, Hattic and Aramaic, against Hurrian, Sumerian, Elamite, Phoenician, Ugaritic, Phrygian and Greek, which have it. A four-vowel Minoan would be ordinary for its area. The first screen compared a Bronze Age language with living ones, which is the temporal bias the authors of BDPROTO themselves flag.

And a measurement on the open question. If Minoan had four vowels, the Mycenaeans would have added the o-series when adapting the script, and its signs would show fewer Linear A correspondences than the other series. Over the 82 Linear B syllabograms: the i-series has a known correspondence in 100% of cases, the u-series in 90%, the e-series in 69%, the a-series in 67%, and the o-series in 35%, 6 of 17, p = 0.017 against the overall rate of 63%. The shortfall is specific to the o-series and is not explained by the average rate, which makes those eleven signs candidates for Mycenaean innovation.

Accordingly, no argument in this chapter rests on Minoan lacking /o/. The measurement is reported as a fact about the script's attested signs, and the question of the language's vowel system is left where the field has it.

Over 3,085 syllabic positions of the intact corpus, eight signs of the o-series have zero occurrences (jo, mo, no, qo, so, wo, do, zo); o, po and to are rare (8, 7 and 14); ro is frequent (86) and ko is not (11). The series as a whole is 4.7% of the vowels of Minoan against 26.7% of Mycenaean, and 0.9% if ro and ko are set aside as Finkelberg (2001) suggests they may be. The reverse holds for u: 17.4% against 7.7%.

The doublets Lejeune observed also survive a null for the first time. Of the ninety intact units ending in -u, sixteen have an exact -o counterpart in the Linear B lexicon (a-ru/a-ro, di-de-ru/di-de-ro, ka-ru/ka-ro, qa-qa-ru/qa-qa-ro, si-tu/si-to and eleven more), against 8.0 expected when the final vowel is replaced at random by a, e or i (500 repetitions, maximum 14, p < 0.002).

This is the first hard phonological constraint the work produces, and unlike everything else in this chapter it constrains rather than closes: any language proposed for Linear A must account for a vowel system that barely uses o.

#### 3.9.2 2 The profile across ten languages

The constraint can only be applied to languages written in a system that distinguishes o, which excludes the seven cuneiform candidates [12]: their transliterations return 0.0% as a property of the script. Distance is total variation against Minoan.

The poverty of o excludes Greek, as Lejeune concluded in 1956 [10]: Mycenaean has the highest o in the table. But it does not place Minoan in Anatolia. Within that family the o runs from 0.0% in Lycian and Lycian B to 24.3% in Pisidian: it is a feature of individual languages and not of the branch. Sharing it with Lycian brings Minoan no closer to Anatolia than to Etruscan, which shares it and is not Anatolian.

#### 3.9.3 3 Reduplication points the other way

Lejeune's second observation [10] also holds. Minoan repeats adjacent syllables in 4.4% of its intact units against 2.3% in Mycenaean, p = 0.0038. Measured the same way across the Anatolian corpora: Lycian 6.3%, Minoan 4.8%, Lydian 1.2%, Carian 1.2%.

That is the exact reverse of the vowel profile. On vowels Lydian is nearest and Lycian furthest; on reduplication Lycian shares the feature and Lydian does not.

A third feature was attempted and does not calibrate: the profile of endings shared by three or more roots. Its null is not comparable across writing systems, since shuffling syllables and shuffling letters generate spurious endings at very different rates (Lycian returns 0.44 and Pisidian 2.87 on 165 words). It is declared unusable for comparison between scripts.

#### 3.9.4 4 The disagreement is general, not local

One feature points to Lydian, another to Lycian, a third to nothing. That is not a defect of these particular measurements: structural and typological features are known to carry areal signal as readily as genealogical signal, which is why almost all quantitative phylogenetics in linguistics codes lexical cognates instead.

The problem has been measured directly. A study of July 2026 in *Entropy* [13] ran 29 structural features over 28 Transeurasian languages with a pipeline first calibrated on Indo-European. At the depth involved, the signal separated broad typological profiles but did not reconstruct internal relationships: Bayesian and distance-based inference returned near-complete polytomies, no branch reached a posterior probability above 0.75, and an unrelated Indo-European control language, included precisely for the purpose, was pulled into the family.

Our own data says it in one line. Etruscan sits at 0.111 from Minoan on the vowel profile and is the worst of the thirteen candidates under the anchored lexical search, at z = −3.2. A shared phonological profile is area or typology; it is not kinship.

#### 3.9.5 5 The shape of the whole

The two criteria available to this field fail in mirrored ways, and both failures are now measured.

By lexicon, there is too much evidence. Under the permissive phonology a CV syllabary imposes, 89.1% of Linear A units find a Semitic comparandum, Greek finds one 79.7% of the time, and fictitious grids reach 97.1%. Optimising for lexical fit finds grids 2.4 times better than the standard one, changing 37 of 38 free signs.

By typology, there is too little. Three features give three answers, and the discipline that formalised the method reports the same result on a family with a corpus orders of magnitude larger than ours.

What is missing in both cases is not evidence but a rule for weighing it, and the information count of §7 says why no such rule can be built from this corpus alone: specifying a decipherment costs 587 bits and the corpus supplies at most 236.

#### 3.9.6 Two further phonological facts, and a power analysis that licenses them

Two measurements complete the phonological profile, and both are reported with the power analysis that makes their negatives readable.

Minoan has no vowel harmony. Adjacent syllables share their vowel in 27.1% of 1,094 pairs against 27.8% expected when the syllables are shuffled (p = 0.74). Linear B, the same script writing Greek, does have it: 29.3% against 24.0%, p < 0.0001. And the negative is not a want of material: subsampled to the 1,094 pairs Linear A has, Linear B's harmony is detected in 30 of 30 subsamples. The absence is real.

Adjacent syllables in Minoan predict each other less than in Greek. The mutual information between the consonants of adjacent syllables exceeds its null by 0.081 in Linear A (p < 0.005) and by 0.226 in Linear B at the same corpus size (ten samples, range 0.195 to 0.274). The dependency exists in both and is about three times weaker in Minoan.

Taken with the o-series and reduplication, four phonological facts now describe the language without naming it: a vowel system poor in o and rich in a and u, reduplication at twice the Greek rate, no harmony, and weak dependency between adjacent syllables. None requires a candidate language, each has its control, and any future reading must satisfy all four.

One methodological caution belongs with them. An earlier version of the dependency measurement compared Minoan with eight candidate languages syllabified by us from their alphabetic forms. All eight returned identical vowel dependency of exactly zero, which is what a function that assigns vowels by construction will always produce. Only corpora syllabified by their own scribes, Linear A and Linear B, are comparable here, which is why the comparison above uses no others.

#### 3.9.7 The reason, in an information count

Specifying a decipherment means assigning a value to each free sign. The complete syllabary has 110 signs, sixteen anchored by the place names shared with Linear B, leaving 94 free. Assigning each a value from a grid of 74 costs 94 × log₂(74) = 584 bits, plus 3.3 bits for choosing the language among ten: 587 bits.

The corpus supplies less. A unit "working" is an event that occurs 73.8% of the time by chance, so it carries −log₂(0.738) = 0.438 bits. Over 552 intact syllabic units the theoretical maximum is 242 bits, and since each sign occurs in sixteen units on average the constraints are not independent; counting only the units that fix a previously unseen sign gives 236 bits.

Deficit: 351 bits. Of the order of 2³⁵¹ distinct assignments fit the corpus as well as any other.

That number is the explanation of the century described in §1. Proposals are serious enough; the difficulty is that with 351 bits of slack, any sufficiently worked hypothesis produces a coherent lexicon. Packard demonstrated in 1974 that nine fictitious decipherments could be built; this is the figure that explains why he could.

It also prices the only way out the field already knew. Each sign anchored from outside removes 6.2 bits. Closing the deficit would take some 57 anchored signs, and sixteen exist.

#### 3.9.8 Discussion

The measurements above do not refute any particular reading of Linear A, and it would be the same error to claim they do as the error they document. A correct reading would also produce many matches; what fails is the inference from many matches to correctness.

What is established is narrower and firmer. The criterion by which these proposals are judged does not discriminate: fictitious grids match as well or better; optimisation prefers grids that keep none of the Linear B values; and requiring form and meaning together returns nothing across the three languages for which a glossed lexicon exists. Anyone advancing a decipherment of Linear A on lexical grounds is now obliged to place it on this scale.

Three things remain outside the reach of these instruments and should be said plainly. Whether Minoan belongs to a given family is decided by comparative morphology, which this work does not do. Whether the phonology of the script was permissive or strict is a hypothesis about the writing system on which 243 bits depend and which no measurement here settles. And what any unit means, as opposed to what it does, is not fixed by distribution at all.

## 4. Five candidates for Minoan names on plant names

### Summary

Greek formed place names and personal names on plant names, and several of those plant names are themselves pre-Greek. This note asks whether the names of the Minoan archives do the same, and measures the criterion it uses to answer.

Three sign groups are examined: KU-PA against *kyparos* / *ku-pa-ro* (galingale), KU-MI-NA-QE against *ku-mi-no* (cumin), and KI-KI-RA-JA beside Neumann's KI-KI-NA (sycomore fig). Each is established first by distribution, position in the document, sealing group, attested derivatives, co-occurring commodity logogram, and only then compared in form, since a method that starts from sound has no discriminating power in this corpus: read with Linear B values and reduced to consonant skeletons, 47% of Linear A groups are attested in Mycenaean Greek, 43% in Hittite, 62% in Ugaritic, 70% in Hebrew and 50% in Etruscan, and the highest ratio belongs to the largest lexicon.

The criterion these three readings rest on has now been measured, and it does not reach significance. Of the 661 intact groups in the corpus, 269 (41%) satisfy the distributional condition, 504 (76%) the derivative-or-logogram condition, and 165 (25%) both. Adding root agreement with a list of twenty plant names, five groups satisfy all three: KU-PA, KU-MI-NA-QE, KI-KI-RA-JA, KA-NA and KI-TA-I. Against a null of false plant names built from real syllables of the corpus, the mean is 1.74 and the maximum 9 in two thousand permutations, giving p = 0.0515.

Two consequences follow, and they govern this version. The three readings are presented as candidates with their measurement declared, not as identifications. And KA-NA and KI-TA-I, which satisfy exactly the same criterion, are added to the list: selecting three of five without a prior rule would be the kind of post-hoc choice this work measures in other proposals.

Keywords: Linear A, Minoan, onomastics, phytonyms, null models, Haghia Triada.

---

### 4.1 The question, and why it has not been asked

That Greek formed toponyms and anthroponyms on plant names is well known: Kyparissos and Kyparissia (κυπάρισσος, cypress), Marathon (μάραθον, fennel), Selinous (σέλινον, celery), Rhodos (ῥόδον, rose), Kydonia (the quince), and among personal names Hyakinthos, Narkissos, Daphne. Several of those plant names are themselves pre-Greek (Beekes 2010; Furnée 1972), and some appear already in Mycenaean as toponyms (*ku-pa-ri-so* at Pylos) or as commodities (*ku-pa-ro*, *ku-mi-no*, *sa-sa-ma*, *ko-ri-ja-do-no* in the Ga series at Knossos, Ge at Mycenae and Un at Pylos).

For Linear A the only accepted identification of a plant word is Neumann's (1960): KI-KI-NA in HT 88 as the sycomore fig (Greek κίκι, Egyptian *kꜣkꜣ*), supported by the group following the fig logogram (NI · KI-KI-NA 7). Younger (2024a) records dozens of groups in his lexicon as "name in a list", "heading" or "place name?" without proposing etymologies. Jiménez Delgado (2024) has collected the pre-Greek names in *-to* of Linear B and compared them with Linear A sign groups. The question of whether the names of the Minoan archives contain plant words has not been posed as such.

### 4.2 The form cannot come first

The interpretation of Linear A sign groups rests on a small number of secure identifications obtained from internal analysis: KU-RO "total" and KI-RO "deficit" (Duhoux 2011), a set of transaction terms and headings (SA-RA₂, KA-PA, A-DU; Schoep 2002; Montecchi 2010), and the toponyms and anthroponyms that reappear in the Linear B of Knossos (Godart 1984). Identifications proposed on phonetic grounds alone have not gained acceptance, for the reasons set out by Duhoux (1989).

That observation can be quantified. For the 729 Linear A sign groups with at least two consonants, read with the values of their Linear B homographs and reduced to a consonant skeleton over a common inventory of thirteen classes, the proportion of skeletons attested in a comparison lexicon is 47% for Mycenaean Greek, 43% for Hittite, 62% for Ugaritic, 70% for Hebrew and 50% for Etruscan; against each lexicon with its consonants permuted, the ratios are 1.47, 1.46, 1.50, 1.68 and 1.57. No comparison language stands out, and the highest ratio belongs to the largest lexicon.

The procedure followed here is therefore the one that produced KU-RO: the function of a sign group is established from its behaviour in the documents, and external comparison is admitted afterwards, as corroboration. Two additions are made. Each distributional claim is tested against a permutation null; and the same tests were calibrated on Etruscan, where they recover the genitive before *clan* (58% of words against a base rate of 26%) and the northern distribution of the marked sibilant.

### 4.3 The groups that satisfy the three conditions by chance

For a match to count, the first version of this work required three conditions: root agreement in the first two syllables with an attested plant name; the distribution of a party (heading a document, or appearing on a roundel of the sealing group that pairs sign group with commodity logogram); and an attested derivative or a coherent co-occurring logogram.

An argument that rests on individual cases satisfying three conditions must measure what chance produces under those same conditions. That test was missing.

Over the 661 intact groups of the corpus:

| Condition | Groups | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution of a party | 269 | 41% |
| Derivative or co-occurring logogram | 504 | 76% |
| **both** | **165** | **25%** |
| **all three**, with a list of twenty plant names | **5** | 0.8% |

The five are KU-PA, KU-MI-NA-QE, KI-KI-RA-JA, KA-NA and KI-TA-I.

Against a null of false plant names built from real syllables of the corpus, preserving the number of plant names and the length of the root, the mean is 1.74 and the maximum 9 in two thousand permutations. The observed value of five gives p = 0.0515.

The three conditions do not reach significance. The excess is nearly threefold and the three groups proposed in the first version are among the five the criterion selects, but a criterion whose p stands at 0.0515 does not license an identification.

An earlier control pointed the same way and is repeated here. Screened against two plant-name lists, one of sixty and one of seventy-nine, the full vocabulary of 799 groups gives 91 root matches against 53.1 expected with the first list, but 97 against 73.7 with the second: the excess of the shorter list concentrates in the KU-PA family and disappears when the list is broadened. The same screen applied to the Linear B corpus (5,597 groups) produces 383 matches, most with Greek anthroponyms unrelated to plants (*ke-re-te-u*, *ko-re-te*), confirming that two-syllable agreement alone carries no weight.

### 4.4 The five candidates

KU-PA. Against *kyparos* / *ku-pa-ro*, galingale. Attested with the derivatives KU-PA-JA and KA-KU-PA, and on roundels of the second Haghia Triada sealing group, where KA-KU-PA is stamped by CMS II,6 nº 11 with logogram \*303 (three impressions) and by nº 21 with \*629 and \*624. In that group the sign group is the party and the logogram the commodity, since one seal accompanies eight different groups. Younger (2024b) reads KA-KU-PA at HT 16 as the person responsible for the contributions of \*303, which is compatible.

KU-MI-NA-QE. Against *ku-mi-no*, cumin. On roundel HT Wc 3014 with the goat logogram, stamped by CMS II,6 nº 77. A qualification is due here to an observation by J. M. Jiménez Delgado (University of Seville): the final -QE is unexplained. Seven groups of 782 types end in *-qe* (0.9%), only two have their root attested separately, and against a null of shuffled syllables those two pairs give p = 0.086, so *-qe* does not hold as a productive suffix, unlike *-ja*, which does with seven pairs (p = 0.020). And KA-PA-QE is telling: its root KA-PA is a Cretan toponym (Uchitel 2002-2003) heading six Haghia Triada tablets, so KA-PA-QE reads naturally as "and KA-PA". If Minoan had an enclitic copula comparable to Mycenaean *-qe*, KU-MI-NA-QE would be the word *ku-mi-na* with a particle attached and not a name. The case satisfies two of the three conditions and the third is open.

KI-KI-RA-JA. Beside Neumann's KI-KI-NA, the sycomore fig. Heads HT 85b, which Uchitel and Finkelberg (1995) had already identified as a heading.

KA-NA and KI-TA-I satisfy the same three conditions and were not proposed in the first version. They are listed here for the reason given in the summary: a criterion selects what it selects.

### 4.5 The scope of the claim

It claims that the question is worth posing and that the criterion for answering it can be measured. It does not claim that any of the five groups is a plant name.

The distinction matters because the field's difficulty with Linear A is not a shortage of proposals but the absence of a stated criterion by which one proposal can be preferred to another. A criterion at p = 0.0515, declared, is more useful than three readings asserted without one.

# Part Three. The price of reading it

*The instrument that the failure of Part Two makes necessary: what a reading proposal must declare, a protocol of ten minimum requirements, a function that turns the question of whether a script can be read into a number, and the accounting of one day spent trying.*

## 5. The instrument: declaration, measurement and the information a corpus holds

### Summary

Proposals to read undeciphered scripts are numerous and are not comparable with one another. None states how many tests it ran before the one it publishes, and the field has no shared criterion by which one proposal can be preferred to another. This chapter offers four pieces of that criterion, developed on Linear A and calibrated wherever the answer is known.

Part I sets out the instrument: what a proposed reading must declare to be assessable, how each claim is measured against an explicit null, and what the instrument returns on scripts that are already read.

Part II gives a protocol of ten minimum requirements, calibrated on four corpora. No published proposal for Linear A meets the fourth: state how many hypotheses were tested before the one that is published.

Part III turns "we do not know whether this can be read" into a number. The decipherability function calibrates on three deciphered scripts and returns, for Linear A, a deficit of 345 bits, 56 signs to anchor from outside, or some 788 further units of text.

Part IV is the accounting of one day spent trying to produce a better reading with the whole instrument, and of what it produced: nothing. It is published because the field's difficulty is not a shortage of proposals but the absence of records of what did not work.

Keywords: undeciphered scripts, decipherment, Linear A, null models, statistical protocol, information theory.

## Part I. Measuring a reading proposal

#### Summary

Proposals to read undeciphered scripts rarely declare what datum would refute them, against what null they were tested, or how many tests were run before the one that gets published. This chapter does three things. First, it sets out ten minimum requirements a proposal should meet to be discussable at all, and for each one shows a case where its absence produced a false positive; eleven of those cases are the author's own, and they are here on purpose, because whoever proposes a protocol should first show where he has failed it. Second, it describes the procedure that implements the requirements: permutation nulls that preserve whatever the claim does not purport to explain, calibration on Etruscan, twelve instruments with a measured error rate, a reading cycle with a novelty gate and batch correction, and two stages with a language model in the loop whose invention rate is measured. Third, it calibrates the whole on four corpora where part of the answer is known, Etruscan, Iberian, Cypro-Minoan, and the archives of Susa and Uruk, reporting in each what it recovers of what is known, what it contradicts, and at what size it stops seeing. The conclusion that bears on Linear A is the contrast: Iberian, with two dozen names from a bilingual, anchors; Minoan, with six consonantal place names, does not. Same instrument, different data.

Keywords: decipherment, null models, calibration, error rate, protocol, Linear A, Etruscan, Iberian, Cypro-Minoan, proto-Elamite.

### 5.1 The protocol

#### 5.1.1 The problem

Proposed readings appear faster than the field can evaluate them, and the million-dollar prize for the Indus script (2025), a corpus whose statistical character has itself been disputed [5, 6] has quickened the pace. The problem is not a want of competence: it is that there is no common minimum of what a proposal must declare for someone else to judge it. Without that minimum, disputes are settled by authority or by exhaustion, and a refuted proposal reappears five years later under another name.

This chapter proposes ten requirements, all elementary in other empirical sciences, and shows for each a case in which its absence produced a false positive.

#### 5.1.2 The ten requirements

#### 5.1.3 A null model for every distributional claim
Any claim of the form "this word occurs with that one", "this affix is productive", "these two document classes differ" needs the permutation that preserves whatever the claim does not purport to explain and destroys only what it does. The wrong null produces positives.

*Own case.* We measured the ascendency/capacity ratio of the document-word network at Haghia Triada and at Susa, and both fell inside Ulanowicz's [2] "window of vitality", an attractive result. With a null preserving row and column sums, the null reproduces the observed value to three decimals. The index was measuring the marginals. Withdrawn.

*Own case.* We measured the compositionality of the Minoan lexicon and obtained 0.12 against 0.02 for a null of shuffled syllables, above Greek at equal size. With a null preserving syllable transitions, the observed value falls inside the null (p = 0.60): it was the phonotactics of the syllabary. Withdrawn.

The rule is that the null must preserve everything that is not the hypothesis. A second part has proved necessary: it is not enough to declare the null, one must declare what claim it covers and what it does not. A null over a set does not cover its members. And a coincidence null, however well built, measures how improbable the observation would be absent an effect; it does not turn a proposal into a certainty nor eliminate apophenia, which no calculation internal to the material can rule out.

#### 5.1.4 A positive control on a corpus with a known answer
Before applying an instrument to the unknown, one must check that it recovers the known in a comparable corpus. If it does not, the instrument's silence is not information.

*Own use.* Calibration on Etruscan (recovers the genitive before *clan*, the chronology of syncope, the geography of the sibilants), on Eteocypriot against Cypriot Greek, on proto-Elamite (recovers Dahl's [4] classification of numerical systems, the institutional header, the names/commodities partition) and on Iberian (recovers Untermann's [3] formants and two known isoglosses).

*Own case, this year.* The triconsonantal root signature seemed the obvious typological test of a Semitic classification. Measured with equal sample sizes across nine languages, Ugaritic, indisputably Semitic, scores lowest of all at 1.03 and Hittite highest at 1.46, with Akkadian at 1.36. The measure does not separate what it must separate, so it says nothing about Minoan. Declared as a closed avenue with its table, so that no one repeats it blind.

#### 5.1.5 A stated error rate for the instrument
An instrument that is not wrong on a known corpus has not been tested. Report where it fails, not only where it works.

#### 5.1.6 A count of the tests run
The probability of a spectacular result grows with the number of tests. A proposal that reports one p-value without saying how many tests preceded it is reporting a maximum, not a measurement.

*Own case, this year.* Six pairs of Linear A units differ by a T-series syllable inserted in second position (A-TA-DE ~ A-DE, DA-TA-RE ~ DA-RE and four more), which is what an infixed -t- stem predicts, at p = 0.033 against a shuffled null. But the same test on the other consonant series gives six pairs for R and six for S. With twelve series tested, the corrected p is 0.40.

#### 5.1.7 Site and support confounders
Archives differ by site, by support and by scribe. A pattern concentrated in one site may be a pattern of that site.

*Own case.* List entries appeared to concentrate by site: 87% in a single site against 32% expected. The list is headed by the two total terms, which occur everywhere, and within Haghia Triada none of the twenty entries concentrates on a scribe (p = 1.00). The effect was the size of the archive, not a property of the class.

#### 5.1.8 A refutation condition
Every reading must state what finding would overturn it, in the form of a datum someone could go and look for.

#### 5.1.9 Prior art with its measurement
A proposal must locate itself against what exists and say by how much it improves on it, measured on the same data.

#### 5.1.10 Separation of function and meaning
What a unit *does* in a document is fixed by distribution; what it *means* is not. Conflating them is the commonest failure in this field, and it is what allows a functional result to be presented as a reading.

#### 5.1.11 A ninth requirement, added this year

When one script is compared with another, the control must be a script of the same kind, and more than one.

*The case that forced it.* Word-final m-syllables occur in 7.1% of Linear A units against 3.1% in Mycenaean, p = 1.5·10⁻⁷, a strong result consistent with Semitic mimation, and the only measurement in this project that ever favoured the Semitic hypothesis. The Cypriot syllabary writing Greek gives 6.2% over 693 words. Two syllabaries writing languages without mimation give 3.1% and 6.2%: the rate is set by the orthographic convention for final consonants, not by the language. Linear A at 7.1% is indistinguishable from the Cypriot rate. Withdrawn seven hours after being obtained.

#### 5.1.12 Two failure modes that no null catches

Circularity in the test's construction. "The unit preceding a logogram never takes a figure" gives 0 of 100 and p = 3·10⁻⁴⁰. It means nothing: if the next token is a logogram then by construction it is not a figure. A p-value of that size should prompt a search for the construction that produced it.

Artefacts of the edition. Looking for sealing batches produced 225 consecutive nodules marked \*301, 151 with KU, 149 with KA. Of the 854 single-sign nodules of Haghia Triada, 825 consecutive pairs share a sign where 135 would be expected if mixed: GORILA orders them by their mark. Any analysis of sequence over that material inherits the ordering. The edition does not flag it.

Both failure modes share a signature: an effect much larger than the phenomenon could plausibly produce. Treat that as a reason to look for the artefact, not as a result.

### 5.2 The procedure

#### 2.1 Data and instruments

The corpus is the 1,720 documents of Linear A in the SigLA/LinearA Explorer transcription, checked against GORILA I-V [10]. Every unit carries a breakage status, because 276 of 783 syllabic units are broken in all their attestations and treating them as words inflates every count.

Twelve instruments, each with a measured error rate, implement the requirements: permutation nulls preserving marginals, transitions or both; a motif-discovery routine against a Markov null; a name-matching routine against a bigram null; a bench that classifies a claim against 53 functional constraints; and a reading cycle with a novelty gate and Bonferroni correction by batch.

#### 5.2.1 Calibration on Etruscan

The instrument recovers the genitive before *clan*, the chronology of syncope and the geography of the sibilants, and fails at exactly the size where the Etruscan corpus falls below Linear A's, which is what makes its silence on Linear A informative rather than empty.

#### 5.2.2 Method negatives

Reported in full because they bound the instrument: Gromov-Wasserstein alignment (2 of 50 correct with the same language split in halves at 455 words), the triconsonantal signature (§1.2.2), free-value search with control (no candidate below control), and cognate-based alignment with published tools that proved not to reproduce their own reported figures.

#### 5.2.3 A case: how the procedure produced, then corrected, a reading

U-NA-KA-NA-SI was filed as the term naming the offering in the libation formula. Re-examined under requirement 1.2.1, the claim rested on a single hyphenated token in one inscription, SY Za 2, not present in the machine-readable transcription, with five attested variant forms. The hyphen is an editorial convention and was shown to be invertible. The entry is now disputed, with its refutation condition: the GORILA IV page for SY Za 2.

### 5.3 The instrument where the answer is known

Four calibrations on corpora where part of the answer is known. Each reports what is recovered, what is contradicted, and the size at which the instrument stops seeing.

#### 5.3.1 Etruscan: function is predictable where meaning is not

Etruscan is read and only partly understood: the vocabulary with established meaning runs to about a thousand entries, and the overwhelming majority of texts are short funerary inscriptions whose content is onomastic. The question posed here is not what the disputed words mean but what class of word they are, which distribution can decide.

Data. The Larth corpus (Vico 2023), 7,139 records with city, date and translation where one exists, setting aside 26 Umbrian texts detected by vocabulary; the ETP_POS lexicon with 1,122 entries and part of speech. Orthographic normalisation raises the known words present in the texts from 316 to 349. Features: initial and final position, single-word inscription, neighbours by known class, ten suffixes, frequency, dispersion by city, and adjacency to a known praenomen. Five-fold cross-validation and a shuffled-label null.

The classifier learns function. Accuracy 0.70 against 0.26 with shuffled labels; recall by class 0.78 (name), 0.54 (noun), 0.67 (verb), 0.83 (particle). The heaviest features are position in the onomastic formula and the verbal suffixes in -ke/-χe.

An unintended validation. The working file omits common words, so terms whose meaning the field does have appear as "unknown". Without knowing it, the classifier is right on eight of ten checkable cases: *teke* (verb, 0.98), *larke* and *fulnike* (verbs, past in -ke), *ein* (particle, 0.75), *θui* (particle), *suθi* and *zilaθ* (nouns), *mlaχ* (noun or adjective). It fails on *mulu* "gave" and *lupu* "died", participles in -u, whose suffix was not among the features. That is the model's own indication of what it lacks.

The candidates. Of 154 words unknown to the field with frequency ≥ 3, nine are not names. Three merit examination: *θapikun* (Populonia, Po 4.4), in predicate position after the relative particle *inpa* and with a derivative in the same inscription, *θapintaś*, which satisfies two of the three conditions of the procedure; *fanu* (Cippus Perusinus), between the particle *eθ* "thus" and the subject *lautn* "family", with the classical comparison to Latin *fanum* but no derivative and no bilingual, one condition and a half; and *aknanasa*, which examination withdraws: it is the participle *acnanasa* "having begotten", translated in the corpus itself, and its classification as a noun is a model error of the same kind as *mulu* and *lupu*.

Limit. The open corpus is noisy (OCR, Umbrian, name glosses in place of translations) and the reference edition is not available in tabulated form. The editors' own doubts sit in the long texts (*θelu*, *parχ*, *munis*, *θlu*, *θup*, *sela*, *iluku*, *cuieskhu*), each with one to three attestations: insufficient for the procedure, and the natural target when the full edition is available.

#### 5.3.2 Iberian: the corpus that anchors

The Iberian semi-syllabary has been read since Gómez-Moreno and the language is still not understood. What is established is a set of regularities: a repertoire of formants combining in pairs to make anthroponyms (Untermann 1990), recurrent suffixes, a numeral system recognised by comparison with Basque (Orduña 2005; Ferrer i Jané 2009), and graphic differences between the north-eastern, Levantine and southern varieties. These were obtained by inspection and internal comparison, not against null models, so neither their effect size nor their probability under chance is known.

Data. 2,094 Iberian texts derived from the Hesperia database (UCM) in the version published by Luo et al. (2021), of which 1,919 have usable text: 2,677 words, 2,359 types. Zones: north-eastern 1,644 texts, Levantine-southern 269. Chronology and support are in Hesperia but not in this copy; their absence prevents separating register and period, and is declared.

Untermann's formants are the repertoire the null points to. The thirty formants sought as substrings occur 295 times; the null gives 11.1 on average (maximum 20 over 100 permutations). The most frequent are *taŕ* (62), *biuŕ* (37), *atin* (33), *iltiŕ* (31), *śalir* (26). The result is not trivial: it says the sequences Untermann isolated are not arbitrary with respect to the phonotactics of the corpus.

Two isoglosses separate under a null, a third is an artefact. Over 2,090 northern and 574 southern words: final -kí at 28.6‰ in the south against 1.2‰ in the north (p = 3·10⁻⁸), and 30.9‰ against 0 in three-sign endings (p = 3·10⁻⁹); -ḿi at 40.5‰ in the north against 7.6‰ in the south (p = 3·10⁻⁵). Both are the isoglosses Hesperia describes. The third difference detected, final -n (21‰ south against 2.3‰ north), corresponds to a transcription convention for the separator and not to a fact of the language; it is reported so that it is not counted as a result.

The bilingual anchor, measured. The Latinised names of the Turma Salluitana (Ascoli bronze, 89 BC) are the only real bilingual of a Palaeohispanic language. For each onomastic element, how many forms of the Hesperia corpus (2,906 intact) contain it, against 1,000 strings of the same length generated by the corpus's own letter-bigram model. In Latin orthography (*adin*, *gibas*, *bilus*, *balci*, *urgi*) it beats the null 1 of 16, because Iberian writing does not distinguish voiced from voiceless and *Adingibas* is written *atin-kibas*. In Iberian orthography, with the correspondence established by the field, 8 of 23 beat the null (1.2 expected; binomial p = 10⁻⁵) and five survive Bonferroni: *bilos*, *sosin*, *biuŕ*, *balke*, *tautin*. Of Untermann's formant list, 15 of 24. The onomastic system the bilingual reveals is present in the corpus, with a p-value per element, and it agrees with what Untermann isolated by hand.

Four claims of the Iberian field against a null. The Basque-like numerals should compose with one another as in Basque: forms with two distinct numerals, 7 observed against 0.7 expected with ten random forms of the same lengths (p = 0.014). *Śalir* as "silver" (Orduña): with metrological marks in 7 of 13 inscriptions against a base rate of 28% (p = 0.046). *Ekiar* as a craftsman's signature after a name: 1 of 8 as a separate token and 1 of 13 within the same form; it does not hold with this corpus. The funerary formula *aŕe take*: two inscriptions, no null possible. One holds, two are borderline, one falls, the same proportion the procedure yields over Younger's readings in Linear A.

#### 5.3.3 Cypro-Minoan: the nearest neighbour

From the dataset published by Corazza [7], Tamburini [8], Valério and Ferrara (2022), 183 inscriptions are reconstructed with their sign sequence, site and support: 1,386 signs, 155 types. Against a unigram null (same signs, shuffled order), repeated sequences are well above chance: 2-grams 83 against 43.9; 3-grams 19 against 0.9; 4-grams 6 against 0. The corpus has recurrent lexical units, which is the minimum condition for any matching method to have something to work with. The contrast between tablets and other supports has no power (6 tablets, all from Ugarit). This is the calibration closest to Linear A in size, script and period, and the one that most resembles it in what it cannot do.

#### 5.3.4 Susa and Uruk: the same instrument on larger archives

The three archives share what makes them hard and what makes them comparable. None has a bilingual: Linear A is not read; proto-Elamite is not read and its language is unknown; proto-cuneiform is read in part, through the continuity of its logograms and numerals into Sumerian cuneiform. All three are accounting archives of a palace or institution, with the same document type: a tablet recording entries of a quantity of something associated with someone, with a header and sometimes a total. The literature has described that anatomy separately for each, but it has not been measured with one instrument across the three, so it is not known which features belong to early accounting in general and which to each tradition.

The comparison also has a methodological value. The apparatus used here produces in Linear A a series of negative results that were attributed to corpus size: no measurable co-exclusion, no reliability in the network descriptors, no power to separate document types. Applied to sibling corpora five and twenty times larger, it allows one to say for each instrument at what size it begins to see, and so turn those negatives into a power curve.

Haghia Triada, around 1450 BC: some 150 tablets and 150 nodules and roundels, preserved because the building burned in the Late Minoan IB destructions, recording the last year of an administration that allocated grain, oil, wine, figs, livestock and aromatics, with twenty-one scribes working at once. There is no temporal stratification: it is a still photograph.

#### 5.3.5 The four calibrations applied to Linear A

The contrast is the result. Iberian anchors: two dozen names from a bilingual fix values, and the instrument then recovers Untermann's formants and two known isoglosses, with p-values. Minoan does not: six consonantal place names shared with Linear B fix sixteen signs of 110. Same instrument, different data.

That contrast can now be priced. Specifying a decipherment costs 587 bits (94 free signs × log₂ 74, plus the choice of language); the corpus supplies at most 236; each sign anchored from outside removes 6.2 bits, so closing the deficit would take some 57 anchored signs. Iberian has its bilingual. Minoan has sixteen signs.

And Cypro-Minoan gives the size at which the instruments fall silent: at 1,386 signs the recurrent lexical units are still visible, but every contrast between document classes loses power. Linear A sits just above that line on the first count and just below it on the second, which is why its functional inventory grows while its lexical one does not.

### 5.4 The case for doing this now

In August 2026 a complete Semitic reading of Linear A appeared as a preprint with a DOI, an auditable catalogue of 67 readings, and its phonological conversion rules declared explicitly. That last point is a merit and is rare: declaring the rules makes the space of solutions measurable, which is precisely what the companion paper does with it.

The result there is instructive for this protocol. Under the declared rules, 89.1% of Linear A units find a Semitic comparandum, Mycenaean Greek finds one 79.7% of the time, and Packard's [1] fictitious grids reach 97.1%. Simulated annealing over the free signs finds grids scoring 183 against 75 for the standard grid, changing 37 of 38 signs. The criterion by which such proposals are judged does not merely fail to discriminate: it prefers assignments that are almost certainly wrong.

None of that refutes the reading. It fixes where the weight of a reading must lie, which is the whole purpose of a protocol.

Works this protocol leans on. The fictitious-grid control is Packard [1]; the entropy dispute over the Indus script, which is the clearest recent case of a statistic without a control, is Rao [5] against Sproat [6]; the fraction values are Corazza and colleagues [7]; and the limits of palaeography as a criterion are set out by Salgarella and Judson [9].

## Part II. A minimum protocol

#### Abstract

Proposals for reading undeciphered scripts appear faster than the field can evaluate them, and the reason is not a shortage of expertise but the absence of a shared minimum of what a proposal must state to be assessable at all. Drawing on the experience of applying a single procedure to five corpora with known or partly known answers (Etruscan, Eteocypriot, Proto-Elamite, proto-cuneiform and Iberian) and to one that has none (Linear A), and on our own withdrawn results, this chapter proposes ten requirements: a null model for every distributional claim; a positive control in a corpus with a known answer; a prediction about unseen text; a declaration of how many tests were run, with correction for multiple comparisons; verification of the data in the primary edition; publication of negatives with their figures; an account of the ending of any sign-group identified with a word of another language, or an explicit statement that it is unaccounted for; and, before any absence is interpreted, a check that the instrument could have seen what is said to be missing. None of the ten requires computational methods, and none demands that a proposal be correct: they demand that it be evaluable. For each requirement a case is given in which its absence produced a false positive, and twelve of those cases are the author's own, all withdrawn with their figures. Two further failure modes are described that no null model catches, because they are properties of how a test is built or of how an edition is ordered rather than of the data.

Keywords: undeciphered scripts, method, null models, reproducibility, Linear A, Indus script.

#### 5.4.1 The problem

Reading proposals appear faster than the field can evaluate them, and the million-dollar prize for the Indus script (2025) has accelerated the pace. The problem is not a lack of competence: it is that there is no common minimum of what a proposal must declare for another to judge it. Without that minimum, discussion is settled by authority or by exhaustion, and a proposal that is wrong for a demonstrable reason survives beside one that is wrong for reasons nobody can articulate.

This chapter proposes no method and evaluates no one else's proposal. It proposes ten requirements, all elementary in other empirical sciences, and for each shows a case in which its absence produced a false positive. Twelve of the cases are the author's own, and they are there deliberately: whoever proposes a protocol should first show where he has failed it.

#### 5.4.2 The ten requirements

#### 5.4.3 A null model for every distributional claim
Every claim of the type "this word occurs with that one", "this affix is productive", "these two document classes differ" needs a permutation that preserves what the claim does not purport to explain and destroys only what it does. Any null will not do: the wrong null produces positives.

Own case: we measured the ascendency-to-capacity ratio of the document-word network at Haghia Triada and at Susa, and both fell within Ulanowicz's "window of vitality", an attractive result. With a null preserving row and column sums, the null reproduces the observed value to three decimal places. The index was measuring the marginal distributions. Withdrawn.

Own case 2: we measured the compositionality of the Minoan lexicon (whether long words decompose into recurrent elements) and obtained 0.12 against 0.02 for the shuffled-syllable null, above Greek at equal size. With a null preserving syllable transitions, the observed value falls within the null (p = 0.60): it was the phonotactics of the syllabary. Withdrawn.

Rule: the null must preserve everything that is not the hypothesis. A second part must be added to this requirement, which applying the protocol to someone else's proposal showed to be necessary. Declaring the null is not enough: one must declare which claim it covers and which it does not. A null over a set does not cover its members, and this is a common case: in the companion paper on toponyms, fifteen cases jointly exceed the null and none stands on its own, which is stated there expressly. And a null on coincidence, however well built, measures how improbable the observed would be were there no effect; it does not turn a proposal into a certainty, nor does it eliminate apophenia, the bias of seeing structure where there is none, which no calculation internal to the material can rule out. The gap between what a null measures and what is concluded from it is as real a failure as not running one.

#### 5.4.4 A positive control in a corpus with a known answer
Before applying an instrument to the unknown, one must check that it recovers the known in a comparable corpus. If it does not, the instrument's silence is not information.

Our use: we calibrated on Etruscan (it recovers the genitive before *clan*, the chronology of syncope and the geography of the sibilants), on Eteocypriot against Cypriot Greek (it separates the two languages by their endings), on Proto-Elamite (it recovers Dahl's classification of the numerical systems, the institutional heading and the name/commodity partition) and on Iberian (it recovers the formulaic elements).

Counterexample from the field: most reading proposals for Linear A and for the Indus script report no positive control, so it is not known whether their method would see the answer if it were in front of them.

#### 5.4.5 A prediction about unseen text
A reading that only explains the texts already known cannot be evaluated. The proposal must say what it expects to find in the next inscription and what would refute it.

Our use: the reading of the KU-PA family predicts that its members will go on appearing with the cyperus logogram and with dry commodities, that a new roundel with KA-KU-PA will carry that logogram or a ligature of it, and that the family will not appear as the recipient of liquids. The protocol for what will be done with the Knossos sceptre when it is published is pre-registered before the text is known.

Note: the prediction does not require that the text appear; it requires that it be written down beforehand.

#### 5.4.6 How many tests were run, with correction for multiple comparisons
A procedure that tests forty hypotheses finds two below 0.05 by chance. The proposal must declare the denominator and correct within each family of tests.

Own case: of eleven suffixes tested in Linear A, three exceed the threshold uncorrected and none survives Bonferroni; we report it as such, and the result we sustain is not "these three suffixes are productive" but "there is a positional signal of morphology that none of the suffixes individually demonstrates".

Counterexample from the field: screens for lexical coincidence between an unread script and an extensive dictionary, which are in effect thousands of simultaneous tests, almost never declare that number.

#### 5.4.7 Verify the datum in the primary edition
Derived transliterations, including excellent ones, introduce errors that propagate.

Own case: one of our supports for reading KU-PA as a commodity was a ligature, \*304+PA-KU-PA, on a Haghia Triada nodule. The palaeographic database reads three consecutive signs there: the ligature does not exist. It sustained an argument for two days.

Own case 2: the same source transcribes some fraction signs as whole numbers, which made the arithmetic of several tablets fail.

Rule: every datum sustaining a claim is checked in the edition or in the palaeographic database, and the version used is declared.

#### 5.4.8 Publish negatives with their figures
A negative without a number is not information: "we found no co-exclusion" may mean that there is none or that the corpus is small. With a number, the negative is a measure of power and saves the next investigator the work.

Our use: there is no measurable co-exclusion between Linear A words (zero pairs with expectation above one in 224 documents), and there is in Proto-Elamite with 830 tablets (13 pairs); a network descriptor has no test-retest reliability at these sizes; comparison by sound does not discriminate between candidate languages (all between 1.3 and 1.7 over their own null) either in Linear A or in Linear B, where the same rule produces 383 false positives.

#### 5.4.9 Account for the ending, or state that it is unaccounted for
When a proposal identifies a sign-group with a known word of another language, the comparison usually rests on the stem and leaves the ending untreated. That is where most false positives enter, because a stem of two or three syllables coincides easily and an unexplained ending may be concealing morphology, a particle, or a misplaced word boundary.

The requirement is cheap: state what the ending is proposed to be, or state expressly that it is unaccounted for. It does not demand being right; it demands not being silent.

An own case illustrates it, which is why it is included here. In the companion paper on names formed on plant words we proposed KU-MI-NA-QE as formed on the word for cumin (Mycenaean ku-mi-no, Greek κύμινον, a Semitic loan), on the basis of the shared stem and of its distribution on a sealed roundel, where the sign-group is the party and the logogram the commodity. The vowel -a against Mycenaean -o is no objection, since it follows the correspondence rule measured over 46 pairs (the Minoan final vowel becomes -o in Greek and -a is never preserved). But the ending -QE was left untreated, and a consultation with J. M. Jiménez Delgado (University of Seville) identified it as the problem with the case.

Checked in the corpus, the objection holds. There are seven sign-groups ending in -qe among 782 types (0.9%), and only two have their stem attested separately; against the shuffled-syllable null preserving lengths, those two pairs give p = 0.086, so -qe does not hold as a productive suffix, unlike -ja, which does with seven pairs. And KA-PA-QE is telling: its stem KA-PA is a Cretan toponym (Uchitel 2002-2003) heading six Haghia Triada tablets, so that KA-PA-QE reads naturally as "and KA-PA". In Mycenaean, -qe is the copulative enclitic. If Minoan had a comparable enclitic, KU-MI-NA-QE would be the word ku-mi-na with a particle attached rather than a name, and its two contexts admit that reading.

The case is not refuted; it is left open: it satisfies two of the three conditions, with the third in dispute. Writing it that way is what this requirement asks, and it is what the original proposal did not do.

#### 5.4.10 Before interpreting an absence, check that the instrument could have seen it
A zero is the most tempting observation in a small corpus and the easiest to misread. When something does not appear, there are two possible explanations and only one is interesting: that it is absent because the object did not have it, or that it is absent because the instrument could not see it. The requirement consists in ruling out the second before asserting the first.

Two own examples illustrate it, both detected on applying this requirement retroactively.

First. We claimed that no Linear A sign-group ends in -so, and contrasted this with the fact that Cretan toponyms end in -so in Greek (Knossos, Tylissos, Amnisos). Measuring the full distribution of syllables, it turns out that the syllable so never occurs in the corpus, in any position, and with it seven others of the -o column (do, jo, mo, no, qo, wo, zo). The word-final absence was not a phonotactic feature of Minoan: it was a gap in the transliterated syllabary. The claim was withdrawn and replaced by the comparison of each syllable with its own general frequency, which does measure a positional preference and does not depend on which signs have an assigned value.

Second. Applying by semantic field the functional criterion of a companion paper, names formed on animal words gave zero matches against nine for the plant field. The immediate reading was that Minoan onomastics did not form names on animals, which would be a cultural feature with content. But the search is necessarily made with Mycenaean stems, and in Crete livestock is always written with a logogram and never with syllables: the Minoan words for animals with which to search do not exist. The zero measured the absence of available vocabulary, not the absence of the pattern.

The check is cheap in both cases and consists of the same question: what would have to be in the corpus for the instrument to detect what I say is missing? If that prior condition is not met, the absence is not a datum about the language.

The requirement is therefore stated thus: every claim of the form "X does not occur" must be accompanied by the check that X could have occurred, that is, that the sign, word or category was available to the instrument in the material and the transliteration used. Where that check cannot be made, the absence is reported as a property of the available corpus and not of the language.

#### 5.4.11 When one script is compared with another, the control must be a script of the same kind, and more than one

A rate measured on a script is a property of the script's orthographic conventions as much as of the language behind it. A single comparison witness cannot separate the two.

*The case that forced this requirement, obtained and withdrawn within seven hours.* Word-final m-syllables occur in 7.1% of Linear A units against 3.1% in Mycenaean Greek, p = 1.5·10⁻⁷. Mimation is a diagnostic feature of Semitic, and this was the only measurement in a long project that favoured a Semitic classification of Minoan. The Cypriot syllabary writing Greek gives 6.2% over 693 words. Two syllabaries writing languages without mimation give 3.1% and 6.2%, because Linear B usually omits a final consonant while the Cypriot syllabary writes it with a dead vowel. Linear A at 7.1% is indistinguishable from the Cypriot rate. The effect was real; the inference was not.

#### 5.4.12 Declare the scope of a reading: which archive, which support, which site

A function fixed on one archive is a reading of that archive until it is shown to transfer.

*Our case.* An inventory of functions was built on Haghia Triada, which is 64% of the Linear A corpus, and read as a description of "the Minoan receipt". Measured against its own base rates, the second largest series is a different thing: Khania never sums (0 of 105 tablets carry a summation term against 41 of 205 at Haghia Triada, p = 7·10⁻¹¹), records in fractions, and has no headers or qualifiers of its own. The general claim had to be rewritten as a claim about one archive.

#### 5.4.13 Two failure modes that no null model catches

The ten requirements above concern what a proposal must declare. These two concern how a test can be wrong while satisfying all ten, and both were encountered in a single day.

Circularity in the construction of the test. "The unit preceding a logogram never takes a figure" gives 0 of 100 and p = 3·10⁻⁴⁰. It means nothing: if the next token is a logogram then by construction it is not a figure. The null model is correct, the arithmetic is correct, and the result is empty.

Artefacts of the edition's ordering. A search for sealing batches produced 225 consecutive nodules marked \*301, 151 with KU, 149 with KA, apparently the trace of large sealing operations. Of the 854 single-sign nodules of Haghia Triada, 825 consecutive pairs share a sign where 135 would be expected if they were mixed: the standard edition orders them by their mark. Any analysis of sequence, neighbourhood or batch over that material inherits the ordering, and the edition does not flag it.

Both share a signature worth stating as a rule: an effect much larger than the phenomenon could plausibly produce is a reason to look for the artefact, not a result. A p-value of 10⁻⁴⁰ in a corpus of a few hundred documents should prompt a search for the construction that produced it.

#### 5.4.14 The cost of the requirements

A protocol that nobody applies to themselves is an exhortation. Over one project on Linear A, applying the ten requirements produced fifteen closed avenues and twelve withdrawn claims, each filed with its measurement and its reason. Three examples of what the requirements removed:

- A network index placing two Bronze Age archives inside Ulanowicz's "window of vitality": the null preserving row and column sums reproduced the observed value to three decimals. It was measuring the marginals.
- A compositionality figure for the Minoan lexicon of 0.12 against 0.02 for shuffled syllables, above Greek at equal size: with a null preserving syllable transitions, the observed value falls inside the null (p = 0.60). It was the phonotactics of the syllabary.
- The mimation result of §2.9, which survived seven hours.

None of the twelve was caught by a referee. All were caught by the requirement that the author himself had written down.

#### 5.4.15 The limits of the protocol

It does not require computational methods: a proposal made by hand can satisfy all ten requirements, and many computational proposals satisfy none. It does not require giving up intuition, which is the origin of almost every good reading; it requires separating the moment of intuition from the moment of checking. And it does not require that a proposal be correct in order to be publishable: it requires that it be evaluable.

#### 5.4.16 The protocol applied to two proposals by others, and calibrated on a language with an anchor

The protocol has been applied to two readings of Linear A that are not ours, without naming their authors, because what matters is the pattern and not the signature.

The first is a decipherment text with seven claims. One partly meets the requirements; two are measurable and fall when measured: the power law offered as proof of "natural language" is obeyed better by the shuffled corpus (R² 0.95) than by the real one (0.81), and the word breaks presented as successes are right 88.5% of the time against a base rate of 86.5%. The other four state no null and can have none.

The second is a public repository generated with a language model that declares Linear A "solved in five minutes" with 92% confidence. Against the corpus: it gives 1,450 inscriptions with Knossos 450 and Haghia Triada 300 (the corpus has 1,720, Knossos 59, Haghia Triada 1,108); it reads "YA-NE" as wine with confidence 0.90, and the unit does not exist; it proposes "formulas" (oil + 10 + recipient) that occur four times in 89; and it assigns to bare syllables meanings such as "divine invocation". The only correct items (KU-RO total, PA-I-TO Phaistos, VIN wine) were already published. It is the extreme case of a proposal that states nothing verifiable, and it shows why two of the ten requirements, verifying the datum in the edition and counting the tests, must precede any null: a null on a unit that does not exist is a calculation without an object.

The same procedure, applied to a language that does have an anchor, is the calibration. Iberian is read and not understood, and has a partial bilingual, the Latinised names of the Turma Salluitana on the Ascoli bronze. Their elements, written in Iberian orthography (which does not distinguish voiced from voiceless stops: Adingibas is atin-kibas), occur in the Hesperia corpus above a null of strings of equal length in 8 of 23 cases (1.2 expected), five after correction; Untermann's thirty onomastic formants, in 15 of 24 tested. In Latin orthography, without the correspondence, 1 of 16. The same instrument applied to the only near-bilingual source for Minoan, the six Cretan place names of the Egyptian lists, anchors nothing (1 of 9, and that one an artefact of a frequent skeleton). The difference between the two languages is one of data, not of method: two dozen names against six, and the instrument says so with a p in each case. That is what the protocol asks of any proposal: to state how much anchor it has, measured, before saying what it reads.

#### 5.4.17 The precedent this protocol is meant to avoid

The need for these requirements is not hypothetical. Rao and colleagues (2009) argued in *Science* that the Indus Valley script encodes a natural language, because its conditional entropy resembles that of languages more closely than that of several non-linguistic systems; Lee, Jonathan and Ziman (2010) published an argument of the same kind for the Pictish symbols. Sproat (2010) refuted both with a larger set of non-linguistic and comparative corpora, showing that none of those measures reliably tells writing from non-writing, and proposed a repetition-based measure that classified the same symbols the other way.

What failed there was not the statistics but the control: the comparison was made against the non-linguistic systems the authors chose, and not against the set that could have brought the claim down. That is requirement 2 of this list, and the case shows its cost: a claim published in the most widely read journal in the world, disputed for years, which a better chosen control would have bounded from the start.

#### 5.4.18 The case for now, and one instance in the open

Three reasons. The volume of proposals has grown with the availability of tools and of prizes. The corpora are digitised, so the ten requirements are now cheap: a null is ten lines of code. And the field has produced, in recent years, examples of how it is done well: the testing against nulls of the tripartite division of Cypro-Minoan, the validation of Ithaca with an experiment among historians, and explicit warnings about the discriminating power of coincidences in small corpora. This chapter only orders what those works already practise.

In August 2026 a complete Semitic reading of Linear A appeared as a preprint with a DOI, an auditable catalogue of 67 readings, and, unusually, and to its credit, its phonological conversion rules declared explicitly. Declaring the rules is what makes a proposal testable, and the test is instructive for this protocol. Under those rules, 89.1% of Linear A units find a Semitic comparandum, Mycenaean Greek finds one 79.7% of the time, and Packard's fictitious grids (1974) reach 97.1%. Simulated annealing over the free signs finds grids scoring 183 against 75 for the standard grid, changing 37 of 38 signs.

That is requirement 2.2 in action: without a positive control, a criterion that prefers false assignments looks like evidence. The proposal is not refuted by this, and nothing here bears on the comparative Semitic morphology it also rests on. What the measurement fixes is where the weight of the argument must lie, which is the whole purpose of a protocol.

#### 5.4.19 Where these requirements come from, and who else has met them

None of the ten is original. Each is standard practice in a field that has had to formalise it, and several have been met in Aegean studies already.

Positive controls and calibration. Corazza, Tamburini, Valério and Ferrara (2022) reclassified the Cypro-Minoan writing system with unsupervised deep learning and validated the method on scripts with known answers before applying it to the unknown one. Assael and colleagues (2022) built their restoration model for Greek epigraphy the same way. Tamburini (2025) surveys the computational approaches and is explicit about which reproduce and which do not.

Verification in the primary edition. Godart and Olivier (1976-1985) is the reference against which every transliteration in this work was checked, and Montecchi (2009) is the study that established how fractions and calculation errors behave in the Linear A documentation. Where the machine-readable transcriptions in circulation disagree with GORILA, this work follows GORILA and says so.

Declaring what a null covers. Duhoux (1978) analysed Linear A linguistically without claiming more than the distribution supported, and his later insistence that resemblance between isolated words proves nothing is the clearest statement in the field of requirement 1.2.8.

Scope and genre. Salgarella and Judson set out the limits of palaeography as a criterion; Davis (2025) surveys what linguistics-based work has and has not established. Steele's work on the Aegean scripts is the standing example of treating writing system and language as separable questions.

And the requirement no one has met. Not one of the decipherment proposals surveyed in the companion paper reports how many tests were run before the one that was published. That is requirement 1.2.4, it is elementary, and its absence is why a century of proposals cannot be compared with one another.

## Part III. How much information a script carries about its own decipherment

#### Summary

Whether an undeciphered script can be read is argued qualitatively. It need not be. A decipherment is an assignment of phonetic values to signs; the cost of specifying one follows from the size of the sign inventory; and what the corpus can supply toward that cost follows from how often a candidate reading succeeds by chance. The difference is a deficit in bits, and it is a property of the corpus rather than of the method or of the investigator. This chapter states the count, calibrates it on three scripts whose answer is known, applies it to three that are open, and prices what each of the open ones would need. Linear B before 1952, Iberian and Etruscan come out decipherable; Linear A shows a deficit of 345 bits, which converts into two currencies a field can act on: either 56 further signs anchored from outside, or some 788 further intact units of text. For the other undeciphered scripts three of the four inputs are published and the fourth, the match rate, has never been measured for any of them; the chapter sets out what measuring it would require rather than supplying it from judgement. The estimate is deliberately generous to the corpus, so a script that shows a deficit under these assumptions shows one under any.

Keywords: undeciphered scripts, decipherment, information theory, Linear A, Cypro-Minoan, proto-Elamite.

#### 5.4.21 The question nobody puts as a number

The million-dollar prize offered for the Indus script in 2025 has a premise: that the corpus contains the answer and what is missing is ingenuity. For some scripts that premise is true and for others it is not, and the distinction is not a matter of opinion. It can be computed from four quantities that any corpus can report about itself.

The field does argue the point, but in words. Whether Linear A is readable, whether the Indus texts are language at all [9], whether proto-Elamite will ever yield: the arguments turn on the size of the corpus and the absence of a bilingual, and they are correct as far as they go. What they do not do is say how much is missing, which is what a field needs in order to know whether to keep computing or to start digging.

#### 5.4.22 The count

A decipherment assigns a value to each sign that is not already fixed. Its cost in bits is

cost = free signs × log₂(possible values) + log₂(candidate languages)

What the corpus supplies is the improbability of its units coming out as words. If a unit matches something in a candidate lexicon a fraction *m* of the time by chance, each successful match carries −log₂(*m*) bits, so

supply = independent units × −log₂(match rate)

and

deficit = cost − supply.

Each sign fixed from outside, by a bilingual, by a shared place name, by a loanword whose source is known, removes log₂(possible values) bits, which prices the deficit in a currency a field can act on.

Three assumptions, all generous to the corpus. Every unit is counted as independent evidence, when in fact units share signs and their constraints are redundant. A match is counted as success when a real decipherment also requires the match to be contextually and morphologically right. And the candidate language is counted as one choice among a handful, when in practice it is an open set. A corpus that shows a deficit under these assumptions shows one under any.

#### 5.4.23 Calibration: three scripts with a known answer

| Script | Signs | Free | Cost | Supply | Deficit | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Linear B (as of 1952) [1]** | 87 | 75 | 485 | 9,966 | **−9,481** | Decipherable |
| **Iberian** | 28 | 5 | 26 | 5,048 | **−5,021** | Decipherable |
| **Etruscan** | 26 | 0 | 3 | 6,110 | **−6,107** | Decipherable |

All three are read, and all three come out decipherable with room to spare. The reasons differ and the count shows which is which. Linear B had a large corpus, three thousand units, and a strict match rate, since Greek either fits a sign group or does not; the twelve signs Ventris had from Cretan place names were enough because the supply was enormous. Iberian has few signs and almost all of them anchored by the Ascoli bronze [2]. Etruscan has an alphabet borrowed from Greek, so the values were never in question and the cost is near zero, which is precisely why Etruscan is read and not understood: the count measures the cost of the values, not of the meanings.

#### 5.4.24 Three scripts that are open

| Script | Signs | Free | Cost | Supply | Deficit | Anchors needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Linear A** | 110 | 94 | 587 | 242 | **345** | 56 |

Only Linear A is reported, and the reason matters. Its four quantities are measured: 110 signs and 94 free from the sign list, 552 intact units from the corpus, and a match rate of 0.738 measured directly against the candidate lexicons. For every other undeciphered script, three of the four are published, the sign inventory, the corpus size and the count of externally anchored signs, but the match rate has never been measured for any of them, because no one has run the test. Supplying it from judgement would make the table a report on the author's estimates rather than on the corpora, and the estimates move the verdict.

Linear A is the near case: 56 anchored signs would close it, against the 16 it has. Its supply is small because its corpus is small and because a CV syllabary matches a candidate lexicon 74% of the time by chance, which is nearly free.

Cypro-Minoan [4] is worse than Linear A despite being the nearest relative in script and period: 183 usable units supply 59 bits against a cost of 504. Any project proposing to read Cypro-Minoan by internal method is working with an eighth of the information Linear A has.

proto-Elamite [5] is not close to anything. With 1,900 signs, the cost of specifying an assignment is twenty thousand bits, and no corpus of the size that survives could supply it. The count says plainly what the field has concluded by experience.

#### 5.4.25 The deficit in two currencies

The same figure converts into either of the two things that could be found.

| Script | Signs to anchor from outside | Or further intact units of text |
|---|---|---|
| Linear A | 56 | 788 |

For Linear A the two numbers are the choice the field faces. Fifty-six anchored signs means a bilingual; 788 further units means roughly doubling the corpus, which means excavation. Neither is a computation: the count says that no amount of method closes the gap, and what would.

#### 5.4.26 The case that would test this, and it has an answer

Linear Elamite was deciphered in 2022 by Desset and colleagues [6], after a century in which the field considered it out of reach. Its published quantities make it the natural test, because they run against the intuition the count is accused of merely restating. It has less material than Linear A, not more: 77 hypothesised signs against 110, and a corpus that stood at 25 inscriptions and 1,731 readable signs in the OCLEI supplement of 2020 [7], against the 7,147 of Linear A.

If the count measures corpus size, Linear Elamite should be further from decipherment than Linear A and it is not. What changed was not the quantity of text but three of the four inputs: the script is a closed alphasyllabary of five vowels, twelve consonants and sixty syllabic values with no logograms, so the space of assignments is smaller; the language was known, attested in cuneiform across two millennia, so the term for the choice of language vanishes; and the silver vessels from Kam-Firuz published in the 2000s supplied royal and divine names, Šilhaha, Eparti II, Napiriša, which is anchoring from outside in the strict sense.

The test is worth running and is not run here. Three of the four quantities are published; the fourth, the match rate, would have to be measured on the corpus, which is available through OCLEI and through the Hatamti database at Liège [8]. If the count returns a deficit for the corpus as it stood before the vessels and no deficit for the corpus after them, it will have reproduced a decipherment the field took a century to reach, and the timing of it. If it does not, the count is wrong and should be abandoned.

#### 5.4.27 The limits of the count

It does not say a script is undecipherable. It says the corpus does not contain enough information to distinguish the right assignment from the many wrong ones [3], which is a different claim and a weaker one: an assignment may be correct and unprovable. It does not price the step from a phonetic value to a meaning, which is separate and which Etruscan shows to be the harder one. And its inputs are estimates: the match rate in particular depends on the target lexicon and on how permissive the phonology is assumed to be, and should be reported with the assumption that produced it.

It also inherits a limit from its own generosity. Because it counts every unit as independent when units share signs, the supply figure is an upper bound and the deficit a lower one. For Linear A the honest statement is *at least* 345 bits.

#### 5.4.28 The uses of the function

A field that cannot say how much is missing cannot tell a hard problem from an impossible one, and cannot tell whether the next decade should be spent on method or on excavation. The count is crude, its inputs are four numbers, and it can be computed for any undeciphered script in an afternoon. Its value is not precision but direction: it converts "we do not know whether this can be read" into a figure, a currency and a target.

For Linear A the direction is unambiguous and the distance is 56 anchored signs. Whether the other undeciphered scripts are at a comparable distance or two orders of magnitude away is exactly what nobody knows, and what four measurable numbers per script would settle.

#### Data and reproducibility
The module and its tests are in the accompanying repository. The Linear A figures are measured in the companion papers; the figures for the calibration scripts are drawn from their standard editions and are stated as estimates.

## Part IV. One day of trying, accounted for

#### Summary

In August 2026 a complete Semitic reading of Linear A appeared [2] as a preprint with a DOI, an auditable catalogue of 67 readings, and its phonological conversion rules declared. This chapter asks two questions of it and one of ourselves. Of it: how much of the claim can be verified from the corpus, and does it survive its own controls? Of ourselves: given a measured functional inventory and a set of verification instruments, can we produce a better reading, and what exactly is missing? The answers are, in order: 59 of the 67 catalogue entries verify against the corpus and the eight that do not are transliteration discrepancies, one of them the author's own correct correction of a published error; none of the four arguments supporting the reading survives its control; and no, we cannot produce a better reading, for a reason that can now be stated as a number. The free signs of the syllabary are 94. Our inventory of 151 functional readings constrains 39 of them. Once the constraint that units inherit from the positions the inventory fixes is counted, 69 of the 94 are reached and 25 remain, and of the 25 that remain, eight occur only on sealing supports, which are marks and not words in the account. Seven signs stand between this corpus and a fully constrained syllabary: 43 bits. One of those seven, \*309, is productive across eight forms on a single tablet and has not yet been analysed.

Keywords: Linear A, decipherment, verification, information theory, functional inventory.

#### 5.4.29 What was verified of the claim, and what it withstands

The corpus facts hold. Of the 67 entries of the catalogue, 57 occur in the inscription cited, 2 occur elsewhere in the corpus, and 4 do not occur at all. Of those four, one is the author's own declared correction of a mis-transcription in the standard reference work [3], and our corpus confirms it; one is a segmentation difference; two are sign-number discrepancies (\*319 against \*904). For a catalogue of 67 entries assembled outside the academy, that is a high rate of accuracy in the corpus facts, and it should be said.

The four arguments do not. Each was measured with its own control.

*The lexical argument.* Under the conversion rules the author declares, 89.1% of Linear A units find a Semitic comparandum; Mycenaean Greek, which is not Semitic, finds one 79.7% of the time; and Packard's [1] fictitious grids reach 97.1%. Simulated annealing over the 38 free signs finds grids scoring 183 against 75 for the standard Linear B grid, changing 37 of 38 signs. The criterion does not merely fail to discriminate: it prefers assignments that are almost certainly wrong.

*Prefix conjugation.* The measure calibrates (Akkadian 1.28, Ugaritic 1.20, against Etruscan 1.08, Mycenaean 0.99, Hittite 0.89) and Linear A scores lowest of six at 0.85, but with nine cases against 10.6 expected the interval runs 0.54 to 1.16. No power.

*The tG stem.* Six pairs of units differ by an inserted T-series syllable in second position, p = 0.033 against a shuffled null. The same test on the other series gives six pairs for R and six for S; with twelve series tested the corrected p is 0.40. Measured, no signal.

*Mimation.* Word-final m-syllables occur in 7.1% of Linear A units against 3.1% in Mycenaean, p = 1.5·10⁻⁷, the one measurement of this project that favoured the hypothesis. The Cypriot syllabary writing Greek gives 6.2%. Two syllabaries writing languages without mimation give 3.1% and 6.2%; the rate is set by the orthographic convention for final consonants. Withdrawn seven hours after being obtained.

And the double constraint returns nothing. Requiring both the vocalised form and the meaning a measured function demands, over 44,855 glossed entries (28,187 Akkadian across all dialects, 2,297 Ugaritic DULAT lemmas [5], 5,599 Sumerian), gives zero exact matches under a permissive phonology and zero under a strict one. Ugaritic is the language Gordon used in 1957 and the nearest relative the current proposal invokes, so this is the most direct available test of the Semitic hypothesis.

What this does not establish. It does not refute the reading. A correct reading would also produce many matches; what fails is the inference from many matches to correctness. And the reading rests in part on comparative Semitic morphology, which these instruments do not do and which a Semitist must judge.

#### 5.4.30 Could we do better? The accounting

The reason for building the measurement was not to referee someone else's claim but to find out whether a verified functional inventory could reduce the space of readings enough to propose one. That question now has a number.

The universe. Specifying a decipherment means assigning a value to each free sign. The syllabary has 110 signs; sixteen are anchored by the place names shared with Linear B; 94 are free. At log₂(74) = 6.21 bits each, that is 584 bits, plus the choice of language. The corpus supplies at most 236 bits through lexical fit, so of the order of 2³⁵¹ assignments fit it equally well.

What one day of measurement bought. The functional inventory rose from 57 readings to 151: 65 fixed unit by unit, plus 86 by a class-level function (the opening position of a tablet is the label of the record and not an entry, p = 8·10⁻³⁴). Those 151 readings include 101 syllabic units.

|  | Syllabic units read | Free signs they touch |
|---|---|---|
| Start of day | 22 | 18 of 94 |
| End of day | **101** | **39 of 94 (41%)** |

The universe shrank, and by a large factor: the signs carrying no constraint at all fell from 76 to 55, which is 130 bits, a reduction of 2¹³⁰.

What is missing, once context is counted. Fifty-five free signs carry no constraint from a directly read unit. But they are not in a vacuum: they sit inside units that occupy positions the inventory has fixed. A unit that opens a tablet is a record label whether or not the unit itself has ever been read; a unit taking a figure in a document whose other entries are read is a list entry; a unit standing before a commodity logogram is in the commodity frame.

Counting that inheritance:

| How the constraint arrives | Units |  |
|---|---|---|
| List entry in a document with read units | 25 |  |
| Opens a tablet (label, by class) | 10 |  |
| Precedes a commodity | 4 |  |
| **total inheriting a function from context** | **39** |  |

Those 39 units touch 30 of the 55 signs. The floor therefore is not 342 bits but 155 bits over 25 signs, and 69 of the 94 free signs now carry some constraint, direct or inherited.

The 25 that remain do not form one class, and separating them changes the figure again.

Eight occur only on sealing supports: \*411 (15 occurrences, all roundels at Khania), \*408, \*333, \*328, \*417, \*905, \*351 and \*810. The sealing system has been shown to operate with twenty-eight marks and to share almost no vocabulary with the accounts (seven crossings against 34.4 expected). A sign that only marks sealings does not need a syllabic value *in the account*: it is a mark, not a word, and it does not stand between anyone and a reading of the archive.

Seven occur on tablets or on inscriptions: \*307 (8), \*314 (6), \*309 (6), \*805 (4), \*410, \*342 and \*350. These are the signs that genuinely remain, and at 6.21 bits each they are 43 bits.

Two of the seven have since been attacked. **\*309 is productive: its six occurrences are all on TY 2 from Tylissos, where twelve of the fourteen entries carry it, in three graphic variants and with nine distinct qualifying sequences. It is counted by pieces and not in bulk, none of its ten quantities takes a fraction, against 34% for the bulk commodities of the corpus, p = 0.017, which places it with persons, livestock and discrete goods; which of those is not fixed, since all its attestations are in one document. \*307** occupies the header frame on HT 27a and HT 89 and qualifies oil at Petras, but the frame claim rests on two tablets and does not survive correction. The other five, \*314, \*805, \*410, \*342 and \*350, have between one and six attestations, no repeated form among them, and are beyond any method that works on recurrence.

The answer to the question, corrected twice. The first accounting gave a floor of 342 bits over 55 signs. Counting the constraint units inherit from fixed positions brought it to 155 over 25. Separating the sealing marks, which are not words of the account, brings it to 43 bits over seven signs. The universe fell today from 76 unconstrained signs to seven: 429 bits.

We still cannot produce a reading, and the reason is exact, because the reason has changed in the course of one day. It is no longer that the corpus withholds too much: 43 bits over seven named signs is not an impassable wall. It is that constraining a sign is not the same as knowing its value. Every result in this work fixes what a unit *does*; none fixes what it *sounds like*, and the step between the two needs a judge the corpus does not contain. The seven signs are the boundary of the first kind of knowledge. The second kind has not been touched, and no quantity of the first produces it.

#### 5.4.31 What it would take, priced

Each sign anchored from outside removes 6.21 bits. Closing the 43 that remain would take seven anchored signs, which is a small number and a hard one: they are precisely the signs no distributional method reaches, because five of the seven occur between one and six times with no repeated form. Partial closure is proportional, and the realistic target is whichever of the seven a new document happens to attest again.

Three sources, in order of what they would buy:

1. A bilingual, or running text in a known language naming Minoan things. This is what Iberian has and Minoan does not, and the contrast is the whole difference between the two cases: with two dozen names from the Ascoli bronze, the same instruments recover Untermann's [4] formants and two isoglosses; with six consonantal place names, they recover functions and no words.
2. New tablets increasing the attestation count of the rare signs. Thirty-two signs at one occurrence need a second and a third before any distributional instrument can see them.
3. Better readings of the existing editions. Marginal, but real: 276 of 783 syllabic units are broken in every attestation, and some of them are the same units in different states.

All three come out of the ground or out of the archive, and none out of computation.

#### 5.4.32 The residue of the exercise

The instrument works, and what it produced is useful: a functional description of the archive of Haghia Triada that does not depend on reading a word, and a scale on which any proposal can be placed before publication. What it also produced, and this is the part that answers the question it was built for, is the boundary itself, stated as a quantity rather than as a feeling.

That boundary cuts both ways, and it should. It says the reading under review cannot be shown correct with this corpus. It says ours cannot either, and we have not attempted one. And it says that the next person to propose a decipherment of Linear A will face the same seven signs, whatever their method and however good their idea, until someone digs up a document that attests them again.

# Part Four. Where this stands

*The state of the question, with a verdict measured for sixteen claims the field currently holds, and the seven walls named with their figures.*

## 6. Linear A in 2026

### Summary

Linear A has not been read, and the reasons given for that are not the same from one author to the next. Reviews of the script list proposals and repeat findings; none states which claims have been tested against a null, which fail, and which cannot be decided with the corpus that exists. This chapter is that account. It reviews the state of the question across phonology, morphology, lexicon, syntax, the writing system and the archive; it reports the measurement of sixteen claims the field currently holds, of which eight are confirmed, five are narrowed, two are refuted and one remains in dispute; and it names the walls the corpus imposes, each with a figure. Two of those walls are new: 76% of the corpus dates to a single destruction horizon, so there is no temporal series to analyse; and 102 identified scribal hands share a mean vocabulary overlap of 0.026, so the corpus is not 552 units of one language but many small and nearly disjoint vocabularies. Together with an information count that puts the deficit at 345 bits, and a decipherment model validated on Linear B that requires half the sign inventory anchored before it recovers anything, they explain a century of failure without appeal to method.

Keywords: Linear A, Minoan, undeciphered scripts, decipherment, null models, state of the art.

---

### 6.1 The case for a review with verdicts

The literature on Linear A is large and its findings circulate by citation. KU-RO means total; SA-RA₂ and TE are mutually exclusive and therefore denote opposite transactions; the entries of HT 86 and HT 95 form a connected group; Minoan barely uses the o-series. Some of these are right, some are right in a narrower sense than stated, and at least one is an inference from a fact that carries no information.

A caution on the o-series. The scarcity of o-series signs in Linear A is measured here and is not in dispute. What it means is. Four readings stand in the literature and none is ours: that Minoan lacked /o/, in a three-vowel system (the traditional reading, treated by Packard 1974 and by Palaima and Sikkenga 1999); that Minoan had *more* than five vowels (Duhoux 1989, 72-73); that e and o are secondary developments from an original three-vowel system by contraction of i-diphthongs (Davis 2014, 240-241); and, most recently, that the scarcity is due to chances of attestation given how few o-series correspondences are known at all (Meissner and Steele, a position Judson calls persuasive).

Two measurements of our own bear on it, and the second corrects the first. Screened against PHOIBLE, which holds living languages, 112 of 3,020 inventories have a, e, i and u and lack /o/, 7.8% of those with all four, and none is in the eastern Mediterranean. That suggested a Minoan without /o/ would be typologically exceptional.

Screened against BDPROTO, which holds 212 ancient and reconstructed languages, the conclusion reverses: 21% of ancient languages with three or more vowels lack a vowel of o quality, nearly three times the living rate, and seven are of the Near East and Aegean, Hittite (a, e, i, u), Akkadian (a, e, i, u), Palaic, Cuneiform Luwian, Urartian, Hattic and Aramaic, against Hurrian, Sumerian, Elamite, Phoenician, Ugaritic, Phrygian and Greek, which have it. A four-vowel Minoan would be ordinary for its area. The first screen compared a Bronze Age language with living ones, which is the temporal bias the authors of BDPROTO themselves flag.

And a measurement on the open question. If Minoan had four vowels, the Mycenaeans would have added the o-series when adapting the script, and its signs would show fewer Linear A correspondences than the other series. Over the 82 Linear B syllabograms: the i-series has a known correspondence in 100% of cases, the u-series in 90%, the e-series in 69%, the a-series in 67%, and the o-series in 35%, 6 of 17, p = 0.017 against the overall rate of 63%. The shortfall is specific to the o-series and is not explained by the average rate, which makes those eleven signs candidates for Mycenaean innovation.

Accordingly, no argument in this chapter rests on Minoan lacking /o/. The measurement is reported as a fact about the script's attested signs, and the question of the language's vowel system is left where the field has it.

Nothing in the field distinguishes them, because nothing in the field measures them. A review that repeats the claims reproduces that problem. What follows reports, for each, what a null model says.

The same applies to the negative side. "Linear A is not inflected", "there is no lexical continuity with Eteocretan", "the Cretan glosses preserve Minoan vocabulary" are statements that require a power analysis before they mean anything, and in two of those three cases the power is zero.

### 6.2 The corpus, and two walls

1,720 documents, some 7,150 signs, 552 intact syllabic units, 110 signs. The largest archive is Haghia Triada with 199 documents; Khania has 121, Zakros 44.

Two unit counts recur in this series and are different quantities: 552 intact syllabic units in the whole corpus, which is what the information count uses, and 443 in the accounting documents alone, of which 389 occur on tablets only. Figures quoted below follow whichever is proper to the measurement.

The first wall is chronological. 1,308 of the 1,720 documents, 76%, belong to the LM IB destruction horizon. The corpus is a photograph of one year, not a series. No linguistic change, no palaeographic evolution and no internal dating of variants can be measured, because there is nothing to measure them against.

The second is scribal. 102 hands are identified. Of the fourteen with ten or more intact units, the mean Jaccard overlap of their vocabularies is 0.026, and the highest pair, two scribes of Zakros, share five units out of eighteen and thirteen. Each scribe writes about different things. The corpus is not 552 units of a language but a set of small, nearly disjoint vocabularies, which is why tests of recurrence have so little power.

Those two walls, and not the absence of ideas, are why the statistics of this corpus behave as they do.

### 6.3 Phonology: four facts, each with its control

|  | Minoan | Control | Power |
|---|---|---|---|
| O-series | **4.7%** of vowels, 0.9% without RO and KO; eight o-signs with zero occurrences | Mycenaean 26.3% |, |
| U-series | 17.4% | Mycenaean 7.7% |, |
| **vowel harmony** | **absent**: 27.1% against 27.8%, p = 0.74 | Linear B has it: 29.3% against 24.0%, p < 0.0001 | **100%**, 30 of 30 subsamples |
| Dependency between adjacent syllables | Consonant excess 0.081, p < 0.005 | Linear B at the same size: 0.226 | High |

A vowel system poor in o and rich in a and u, reduplication at twice the Greek rate, no harmony, and weak dependency between adjacent syllables. None of these requires a candidate language. Any proposed reading must satisfy all four, and no candidate in the literature satisfies the absence of o together with the abundance of e, Luwian lacks the phoneme e, and the Semitic languages have o.

### 6.4 Morphology: undeterminable, and for two independent reasons

Kober established that Linear B was inflected by finding ending alternations recurring across stems. On Linear A the same test gives 4 bridges against 6.6 expected, p = 0.86.

That negative says nothing, and the power analysis shows why. Linear B subsampled to the 501 units Linear A has gives a bridge ratio of 0.56 against Linear A's 0.61: indistinguishable. Four inflection statistics, over 25 subsamples each, detect Greek inflection at that corpus size in zero cases.

And a second reason is structural, not statistical. If Eteocretan descends from Minoan and admits final -t and -d, a CV syllabary cannot write those consonants, exactly as Linear B omits the final consonants of Greek. The endings that are measured are the last written syllable, not the end of the word. Part of Minoan morphology is invisible by construction of the script.

Optimising for lexical fit finds grids 2.4 times better than the standard one. By lexicon there is too much evidence; by typology there is too little, and three structural features give three different answers.

### 6.6 Syntax

Prior art: Davis (*Kadmos* 52, 2014) established verb-subject-object order from the libation formula; Younger identified transaction words by position; Schoep classified documents by content.

Measured here, on the accounting document:

Each unit carries its own figure. UNIT→NUM runs at 1.41 over the null in Linear A and 0.70 in Linear B, where units chain before reaching the number. UNIT→UNIT is 0.63 against 1.07. The comparison uses 2,884 transitions against 21,778.

The label opens and the total closes: mean positions 0.300 and 0.737 against a null of 0.506, both p < 0.0005. And the deficit does not close: KI-RO sits at 0.498, the exact centre, p = 0.89. It is a line within the account, not its balance.

Davis's fixed order is confirmed on the 35 stone-vessel inscriptions with three or more words: A-TA-I-\*301-WA-JA opens in 7 of 7 (mean position 0.00), SI-RU-TE closes (0. The fixed order is not a finding of this work: Finkelberg (1990-91) listed the documents that open with the formula and observed that the pattern can hardly be due to mere chance, and Davis and Salgarella followed her. What is added here is the null her wording invites.96). The verb-subject-object identification is not confirmed here, since assigning those categories requires knowing what the words mean.

### 6.7 The writing system

Ligatures bind a syllabogram to a commodity, and the syllabogram is specific to it: 16 of 26 go with one commodity only, against 25% by chance, p < 0.0005. But the ligature does not constrain the phonetic value: tested on Linear B, acrophony of the commodity gives 2 of 10 (p = 0.22) and abbreviation of the adjacent qualifier 4 of 11 (p = 0.41).

The signs Greek did not inherit open their unit more than the rest: 46.8% against 32.3%, with a frequency-matched null of 29.3%, p = 0.002, and they appear on roundels 19% of the time against 5%. What the Mycenaeans discarded was the vocabulary of heading and sealing, not the vocabulary of the count.

Two signs carry both sound and sense, which is what an anchor requires: NI, the fig ideogram, taken from Linear A and read by Neumann (1962) as the initial syllable of a Minoan word preserved in the Greek gloss *nikuleon*; and the goat ideogram with value *mi* against the Cretan gloss *miglas*. Three further ligatures, TELA+KU, OLE+RA, CYP+PA, occur in both scripts with no Greek word to explain them, and are the open question this work leaves to specialists.

### 6.11 The residue of this work

A functional inventory of 151 readings, 67 fixed unit by unit, the rest by the class function of the opening position, each with evidence for, evidence against and a stated refutation condition.

Thirty closed avenues and twenty-one withdrawn claims, each with its measurement and its date. Six of the withdrawals were positives of this work that a control destroyed, among them a z of +6.88 for Akkadian that turned out to be editorial marks in the skeletons, and a sign that opened its unit in 15 of 15 occurrences which were all roundels of a single Khania series.

Thirty-two falsifiable predictions about the Minoan lexicon: for each ligature whose syllabogram carries an inherited value, the Minoan word for that commodity contains that syllable. And two more from the Cretan glosses: if the value of SUS is ever fixed it must be */ma/*, and if VAS is fixed, */a/*.

And a protocol of ten minimum requirements, calibrated on four corpora, whose fourth requirement no decipherment proposal in the literature meets: state how many tests were run before the one that is published.

### 6.12 Limits

Not one phonetic value is fixed here that was not already known. The inventory establishes what units do, never what they mean, and no quantity of the first produces the second: Etruscan is fully read and only partly understood.

Linear A is not thereby undecipherable. It is that the corpus does not contain enough information to distinguish the right assignment from the many wrong ones, and that statement carries its own price.

7. The sign list that had to be built first

7.1 A defect that produced two opposite answers

The corpus does not distinguish a logogram from a syllabogram by shape. NI is the fig ideogram and the syllable /ni/; so are KA, KU, SI, TE, RO, E, O and the numbered signs \*86, \*188 and \*305. A rule based on capital letters confuses them.

That confusion invalidated two syntactic measurements, which returned 77% and 2% for the same datum depending on how the classification was written. Neither number meant anything.
7.2 The criterion is distributional, not formal A sign is a logogram when at least 70% of its occurrences stand alone and, of those, at least 40% are followed by a figure or at least 30% carry a ligature. It is a syllabogram when at least 70% of its occurrences fall inside a hyphenated unit. It is ambiguous when it does at least 35% of each. Applied to the corpus: 8 logograms (GRA, OLE, VIN, OLIV, CYP, VIR, \*304, QA₂), 11 ambiguous (NI, KA, KU, SI, TE, RO, E, O, \*86, \*188, \*305) and 38 syllabograms.

Validated against the logograms the field recognises: eleven hits, no misses, no false positives, and NI comes out ambiguous, which is what it is. The list is in `kuro/signclass.py` with five tests.

8. Six syntactic slots, and a deficit that does not close

8.1 The corrected list, and what it makes visible

With logograms identified distributionally, the document has an order that can be measured. Over 289 documents with four or more elements, 2,884 transitions between unit, logogram and figure, against a null that shuffles the elements within each document: all nine transitions depart from chance.

Compared with 21,778 transitions of Linear B, four are common to both and belong to the genre rather than to the language: a logogram is followed by its figure and by nothing else, and two figures do not chain. Two separate the two archives. In Minoan each unit carries its own figure (UNIT→NUM at 1.41 over the null); in Mycenaean the units chain before reaching the number (0.70).
8.2 The label opens, the total closes, and the deficit does neither Mean position of each function among the units of a document, 0 first and 1 last, against positions drawn at random, 2,000 repetitions: | Function | n | Mean position | Null | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Label | 46 | 0.300 | 0.506 | < 0.0005 |
| Total | 41 | 0.737 | 0.505 | < 0.0005 |
| Deficit | 32 | 0.498 | 0.508 | 0.89 |

The label opens and the total closes, as their function already suggested. The deficit sits at the exact centre. KI-RO is a line within the account and not its balance, which agrees with HT 123+124, where the total precedes the deficit: what was delivered is summed first, and the shortfall is entered as an item.
8.3 One slot with four occupants Between the party and the commodity stand TE (9 of 59 occurrences, base rate 1.5%, p = 2.0·10⁻⁷), **\*307 (4 of 9, p = 5.5·10⁻⁶), SA-RA₂ (5 of 20, p = 8.8·10⁻⁶) and \*516** (2 of 9, p = 7.2·10⁻³).

Two of the four already had a function fixed. The other two are signs with no phonetic value, and sharing a slot with TE and SA-RA₂ is what fixes theirs.

9. The generative half, run at last

9.1 An instrument of rejection, and the half that was never executed

Everything above is built to say no: null models, power analyses, a protocol, a decipherability function. The alignment model of `kuro/aligner.py`, written from Luo and colleagues and validated on Linear B, was set aside because the anchor curve said it could not work with 2.9 anchors. It was never run on Linear A.

Run bare, it ordered the candidate languages by the size of their vocabulary, and put Mycenaean first. It reproduced the artefact this book documents in the field's own criterion.
9.2 The bridge that was missing The model knew none of what had been measured: not the functional inventory, not the vowel profile, not the absence of harmony, not the syllables the system appears to lack. `kuro/measured.py` supplies them as penalties. With it, the best assignment proposes not one absent syllable.
9.3 And with vocabulary and format matched, it still does not discriminate Mycenaean kept winning until its vocabulary was passed through the same crude syllabifier as the others: its words are written in the same syllabary and arrive already in the shape the model expects. Matched, it falls to third, and Hittite leads with the smallest vocabulary of the five. Against thirty false vocabularies built from Hittite's own syllables: z = +1.16, p = 0.100. It does not separate.
9.4 The grid is there and cannot be lifted Ventris did not look for the language. He built the grid from internal distribution and saw at the end that what came out was Greek. That was never tried here.

Over the 48 signs with eight or more occurrences, comparing the contexts of each pair: signs sharing a consonant have more similar contexts (0.7150) than chance (0.6335), p < 0.0005; signs sharing a vowel barely (0.6523 against 0.6343, p = 0.045). **

The structure of the grid is in the corpus, and the consonant organises it.** But it is too weak to lift: grouped into thirteen clusters by context similarity, the consonant series do not emerge. An effect of 0.715 against 0.633 is detected with a thousand pairs and does not suffice to decide where each sign belongs.

This is the power ceiling of Part Three, reached from the generative side.

10. A generator of synthetic Minoan text

10.1 The diagnostic

 A generator was given what had been measured: the

distribution of unit lengths, the frequency of each sign by position, the dependency between adjacent syllables, and the seven syllables the system appears to lack. Nothing else. It produced 501 units, the size of the intact corpus.

It captures, without having been given them, the number of sign types (59 against 57), the entropy (5.470 against 5.440) and reduplication (0.080 against 0.086), which emerges from the syllable dependency alone. **

It fails on one thing: reused three-syllable blocks, 0.050 in the real corpus against 0.014 in the synthetic.** 
### 10.2 The morphology behind the failure

 **5.0% of trigrams recur, against 1.6%

under a null with the same phonotactics. p < 0.0005**, 400 repetitions. And the blocks are morphology: | Pair | Relation |
|---|---|
| DA-KU-SE-NE / DA-KU-SE-NE-TI | Same stem, suffix *-TI* |
| PA-RA-NE / A-PA-RA-NE | Same stem, prefix *A-* |
| A-KU-MI-NA / KU-MI-NA-QE / A-DU-KU-MI-NA | One base, three treatments |
| I-PI-NA-MA / I-PI-NA-MI-NA | Same base, different ending |
| ZU-RI-NI-MA / I-ZU-RI-NI-TA | Prefix and suffix together | ** |

56% of the reused blocks sit at the start of the word, 44% at the end, and none in the middle. They live at the edges, where stems and affixes live. Kober's test does not see this because it requires an ending alternation to recur across three or more stems, and at 501 units that test has zero power. This one looks for reuse of material and finds pairs rather than series. What it establishes is derivational morphology** (prefix *A-*, suffixes *-TI*, *-QE*, *-NE*), not inflection.
10.3 And the power floor is not an artefact of counting If those affixes were productive, units counted as separate words would be forms of one, the effective vocabulary would be smaller, and part of the power floor would be an artefact. It is not. 29 affix families over the 501 intact types against 20.9 expected under a generative null, p = 0.07, and merging them would take the vocabulary from 501 to 473, a 6% reduction that moves no power calculation. The method does recover the families the field knows, JA-SA-SA-RA with its variants and DA-MA-TE with I-DA-MA-TE of the Arkalochori axes, and not enough new ones. **

The power floor belongs to the corpus and not to how it is counted.** 
## 7. A proposal by others, measured

11.1 The case

 Schümann [4] (2026) translates the libation table

IO Za 2 of Mount Juktas in full, with TI-NU as an archaic Dionysos and I-DA-MA-TE as Demeter. The preprint is public with a DOI, which is what allows it to be measured; most proposals for Linear A do not reach that.

Two sign values are revised for the reading to work, A301 to JO and AB013 from ME to ZA, and the chapter does not argue them: it refers to another preprint by the same author and announces a comprehensive treatment of the signary as forthcoming. The lacuna on side C is restored as I-DA[-MA-TE-QE] by reference to a second preprint, the reading of SI-RU-TE to a third, that of I-PI-NA-MA to a fourth. Eight of his own Zenodo deposits are cited, none refereed, and each element of the translation is referred to one of them.

The count of readings tried before the one published is not given, which is requirement 4.

11.2 Three objections from the corpus

 **The base of

SI-RU-TE occurs on its own, and in an accounting document. SI-RU appears twice, once in HT 55a, a tablet**. If *-TE* were a ritual optative, its base would not be in a ledger. And SI-RU-MA-RI-TA₂ has no place in the analysis. I-PI-NA-MI-NA exists, twice, beside I-PI-NA-MA. If *-MA* is a first-person possessive, *-MI-NA* is unexplained. **And *-TE* is not productive.** Of the 44 units ending in *-TE*, six have their base attested separately: 13.6%. Among all final syllables with eight or more cases, *-TE ranks 17th of 44*; *-ME* reaches 43%, *-WI* and *-QE* 25%.

A living grammatical morpheme should sit at the top of that list. The optative on which the last clause of the translation rests is carried by an ending the corpus does not treat as productive.

None of the three requires accepting any affiliation.

12. The longest inscription, published while this was written

Kanta, Nakassis, Palaima and Perna [2] (2025) publish KN Zg 57, an ivory ring from the Anetaki plot at Knossos, and KN Zg 58, its handle: about 119 signs, 84 preserved whole or in part, the longest Linear A inscription known. The ring carries no numerals at all, and its editors align it with the stone libation vessels rather than with accounting records. Its sign groups have few parallels in the extant corpus. The full transliteration is announced for *Anetaki II* and is not in that article, so nothing here can yet be measured against it. Two of its findings bear on this book. The fraction values change. The handle gives six fraction signs in sequence and, in the editors' words, a different sequence of values than those suggested until now. Most measurements here count whether a figure is present rather than what it is worth, and are unaffected; one is not. `astringent_oil_ratio_montecchi_values = 0.67` rests on an assignment of fraction values that the editors of the longest document in the corpus have just questioned, and is flagged as such in the manifest. **

And their argument about ligature can be measured. They observe that the rhyton and the tripod are the only two vases on the ring without a ligatured syllabogram, and explain it by the unmistakable shape of those two ideograms. Over the whole corpus, logograms of unmistakable shape ligature 34.0% of the time and generic ones 50.8%, p = 1.4·10⁻⁵: NI in 76 occurrences, OLIV in 24 and CAP in 5 never ligature at all. 

But VIR ligatures all 54 times it occurs, and a man is as unmistakable a shape as a goat. The criterion sharpens: ligature is absent where the class needs no subdivision**, not where the shape is ambiguous. Of figs there is no need to say which; of men there is, their trade or their standing. It agrees with the generic side too: oil ligatures 83% of the time because there are many kinds, wine only 15%.

The four authors have known the corpus for decades and that VIR always ligatures is no news to them; their article describes a new object in sixteen pages and gives a local explanation of two vases, not a theory of ligature. What is added here is the figure, not the fact.
12.1 A second reading published while this was written Ferrara, Salgarella and Bellinato [1] (2025) read Linear B \*157 as "root": its upper element is the syllabogram *wi*, which they take as an acrophonic abbreviation of *wi-ri-za*, and its lower element as the drawing of a tuberous root. They extend the mechanism to Linear A, where A646 on HT 33 has the same lower element under the syllabogram *pi*, and propose that the Minoan word for root began with that sound. And they separate *ka-po* written out, a generic term for fruit, from the monogram KA+PO, which occurs only at Pylos in perfume ingredient lists and which they read, after Sacconi [3] (1972), as a dried stalk.

This bears on the ligature result of this book in both directions.

Against it: the acrophony of the bound syllabogram was tested here on Linear B, where the answer is known, and held in 2 of 10 cases, p = 0.22. That measurement is why the thirty-six bits once attributed to ligature were withdrawn. Their two readings are argued individually, with contextual support that a general test cannot use, and a rate of two in ten does not forbid two good cases; but a mechanism that works in a fifth of the testable instances is not a rule, and a reading that rests on it carries that rate with it.

For it: they state that Linear A almost never writes a commodity out in full, full writing being reserved for personal names, place names and transaction terms. That division is theirs, and the distributional sign list of section 7.2 reaches it from the corpus alone, which is a check on the method rather than a discovery.

Their closing question, whether the graphic strategies they identify apply to the other unidentified compound logograms of both scripts, is one this instrument could answer with a null.

12.2 The field's own measure of the same ceiling

Petrakis and Steele [5] close *The Wor(l)ds of Linear A* (AURA Supplement 15, 2025) with a section on decipherment prospects, and they name the same limit this book quantifies. Reporting a figure from J.-P. Olivier, they observe that the published Linear A corpus is almost equal in size to the text assigned to Hand 1 of Pylos, the most prolific Linear B scribe, and they name that paucity, alongside the absence of bilinguals, as the main reason the script remains unread.

The limit was theirs first, and the editors of the volume state it plainly. What Part Three adds is a figure to it: 345 bits, 56 anchors, or some 788 further intact units. The comparison and the number say the same thing, and the comparison came first.

They open the section with Younger, posting to Aegeanet on 25 April 2024: "I can read Linear A; I just don't know what every inscription says." That is the distinction this book draws between reading and understanding, put in one line by the person who compiled the transcription everyone uses.

And Davis, in the same volume, argues from affixation patterns that Linear A fits neither Indo-European nor Afroasiatic morphology, which supports the possibility that Minoan is unrelated to any family historically attested in the area. Davis reached that before Part Two was written and from the morphology itself, where this work reaches a compatible result from a null model over thirteen lexicons. The two routes agree, and his is the one that belongs to the field. The volume, in its editors' words, accommodates no new proposals for the Minoan language. Neither does this book.
12.3 Six proposals of the same year, and where each rests Six further readings of Linear A appeared in 2026 or were found while this was written. None is refereed. They are listed because the instrument of Part Three exists to weigh proposals, and a book that measures one of them should say what the others rest on.

Chiapello reads the headers DA-DU-MA-TA and A-DU of HT 95+113bis as Greek, comparing the tablet with the Greek inscription IG V,1 1379, as part of a series arguing that the Minoan language is Greek. That touches a result here: A-DU is measured as a label that opens its document in 12 of 17 occurrences, p = 0.012. The measurement is about function, not language, and is compatible with any reading that makes A-DU a heading, his included. Whalen replies to him in a two-page note.

Vallance Janke, with Perono Cacciafoco, Chiapello and Madhu, reads the libation formula as an Anatolian proto-Greek invocation addressed to Mount Dikte and Mount Ida.

Yao, Perono Cacciafoco and Cavallaro strip the vowels from Linear A read with Linear B values, reduce the units to consonant skeletons, and match them against Ancient Egyptian, dividing sign sequences into words where the corpus marks no division. That is the configuration measured in Part Two: consonant skeletons, a large lexicon and free segmentation, under which fictitious grids find a comparandum for 97.1% of units.

Desherieva takes Minoan to be a dialect of Hattic within Sino-Caucasian and compares reconstructed Minoan words with Proto-Yeniseian. The reconstruction proceeds by acrophony, each syllabogram taken to depict an object whose name began with that syllable, which is the mechanism measured here at 2 of 10 in Linear B, p = 0.22.

Cohen derives the shapes of individual Aegean signs from Egyptian iconography, sign 81 from a four-headed ram god and sign 21 from the wedjat eye.

Five of the six rest on a mechanism this book has measured: acrophony, or lexical matching under a permissive phonology, or both. That is not an argument against any of them individually, and none is assessed here beyond naming where it rests. It is the reason the protocol asks what a proposal rests on before it asks whether the reading is attractive.
12.4 The first wall, raised by the field itself Salgarella and Judson [6], in the proceedings of the fifteenth Mycenological colloquium, test whether the palaeography of the Aegean Linear scripts can carry chronological weight, and conclude that it cannot: graphic variation prevails over standardisation, no clear process of development over time can be identified with certainty, most individual sign variants persist from Linear A into Linear B and within Linear B, and what looks like simplification in the Knossos West Wing may reflect the styles of two scribes rather than a date. Any chronology based purely on palaeography, they write, is to be regarded as thoroughly unreliable.

That result is prior to the first wall stated here. When this work reports that the corpus has no time series, 76% of it belonging to one destruction horizon, it adds a figure to a limit the field had already established and to which palaeography, the remaining candidate for supplying a sequence, had already been shown not to answer.

Civitillo [7], in the same volume, gives the composition of the Cretan Hieroglyphic corpus: of the 331 documents in CHIC, 136 are seals and 57 seal impressions, 122 are on clay and 16 on other media, a picture unchanged by finds since 1996. Her figures are the ones used here for the sixth wall; the wall is hers and the others' before it is this book's.

13. The Phaistos Disc, measured by register

The disc is not Linear A, its 45 signs are stamped rather than incised, and it is a single object with no parallel. It is treated here for one reason: it is the other undeciphered document of Bronze Age Crete, and the instrument of Part Three asks what a text is doing before it asks what it says.
13.1 The question, and what it is not Nothing here bears on the language of the disc, on the direction of reading, or on any of the readings proposed for it. The question is narrower: does its distribution of sign-groups resemble an account, a votive text, or neither?
13.2 Method and data The canonical transcription of Evans and Godart is used, 61 sign-groups of which 58 are intact. Two corpora serve as references: the Linear A sanctuary inscriptions, which are votive, and the Linear A and Linear B accounting tablets. Each document is reduced to the profile of its sign-group distribution, and the disc is placed against each by the same distance.

A warning on sources. The public repository 12aiko55/phaistos-disc-analysis carries a corrupted transcription: every word begins with the sign 02 and side B is duplicated. Any measurement made from it is worthless, and this is noted because the file is easy to find and looks usable.

# What this book establishes

Five parts and thirteen chapters come to five things. **

Linear A is not deciphered here, and nothing in these pages fixes a phonetic value that was not already known. What is fixed, one hundred and fifty-one times, is function: what a sign-group does in its document, never what it means. The two are not the same, and Etruscan and Iberian are read in full and understood in part. 

The criterion by which the field compares languages does not discriminate. Under the permissive phonology a CV syllabary imposes, fictitious grids find a comparandum for 97.1% of units, above comparative Semitic at 89.1% and Greek at 79.7%. That is why none of thirteen candidates reaches z = 3, and it is not a matter of having chosen the wrong language: the criterion admits any of them. 

What is missing has a figure, and three independent routes give the same one.** An information count puts the deficit at 345 bits, which is 56 signs to anchor from outside, or some 788 further units of intact text. The alignment model takes off between seven and ten anchors of twenty and Linear A sits at 2.9. And the grid structure is present in the corpus but too weak to be lifted from it. The editors of *The Wor(l)ds of Linear A* say the same thing in another form, that the published corpus is about the size of one Pylos scribe's output. **

None of the seven walls is removed by computation. Five require excavation or the transcription work of a specialist; two require a find. The generative half of this instrument was built, connected to everything measured, and run: with matched vocabularies and matched syllabification it puts Hittite first and cannot separate it from thirty false vocabularies made of Hittite's own syllables. 

And the field was there first, more often than not.** Sixteen findings of this project turned out to be published already; each is credited where it appears. What is added is the measurement, and a way of judging proposals that did not exist: ten requirements, a decipherability function, an account of a failed attempt, and an appendix listing every test run, the sixty-three that separate from their null and the thirty-seven that do not. Three things of this work's own remain open and can be tested by anyone. That TELA+KU, OLE+RA and CYP+PA are inherited rather than Mycenaean, which would take the anchoring from sixteen signs to twenty-one. That \*307 and \*516 belong to the transaction slot with TE and SA-RA₂. And that Minoan has derivational morphology of the kind the synthetic generator uncovered, a prefix *A-* and the suffixes *-TI*, *-QE* and *-NE*. Thirty-four further predictions are set out in the chapters where they arise, each with the observation that would break it. KU-RO is the Minoan word for "total". This book counts, and the count says what it would cost to read the script, and that nobody can pay it from the corpus that survives.

## B.3 Three classes: measurable, measurable only with new material, not measurable

Measurable with what exists: the classification of the 41 shared ideograms into inherited and invented, if the list of what each abbreviates can be had; the three ligatures TELA+KU, OLE+RA and CYP+PA, which need a paragraph from a specialist; the position of the remaining valueless signs, one by one, with a null stratified by site; and the non-accounting text corpus, 152 documents and 260 units, of which 92% are hapax.

Measurable only with new material: whether Minoan is inflected, whether the grid can be recovered, whether the o-series reflects the language or its survival, and the relation to Cretan Hieroglyphic, whose 331 texts are published as photographs.

Not measurable by construction: the final consonants, which a CV syllabary does not write; meaning as against reading, since Iberian and Etruscan are read in full and underst \newpage
ood in part; and affiliation, while the lexical criterion accepts false grids at 97.1%.

B.4 The one cell that is open

Two signs carry sound and sense together, which is what an anchor requires: NI, the fig ideogram, read by Neumann as the initial syllable of a Minoan word preserved in the gloss *nikuleon*; and the goat ideogram with value *mi* against the Cretan gloss *miglas*.

Three further ligatures occur in both scripts with no Greek word to explain them: TELA+KU, OLE+RA and CYP+PA. If they prove Minoan, the anchoring goes from sixteen signs to twenty-one.

It is the only cell of this map that does not require measuring better.

# How to reproduce this

Everything asserted here is produced by `github.com/BiomeMakers/kuro`, a Python package with 176 tests. The manifest at its root holds every figure in this book with its null model, its number of observations and the condition that would refute it, and `scripts/check_manifest.py` verifies that no figure in the text departs from it. The data. The corpus comes from the Linear A Explorer (R. Hogan, lineara.xyz), which follows GORILA; the Linear B lexicon from the standard editions; SigLA (Salgarella and Castellan) supplies the palaeographic classification. The lexicons of the candidate languages are listed in `data/raw/README.md` with their sources. `data/raw/inscriptions.json` is not redistributed here and is rebuilt with `scripts/fetch_corpus.py`. The order. `scripts/build_derived.py` produces the derived files from the raw ones. `kuro/signclass.py` builds the distributional sign list. `kuro/decipher.py` and `kuro/aligner.py` hold the alignment model; `kuro/measured.py` the constraints it is given. `kuro/decipherability.py` computes the information count. `scripts/nestor_index.py` builds and searches the bibliographic index, and is run before any claim of novelty. The nulls. Every distributional claim here is tested against a permutation null with a stated number of repetitions, which the manifest records alongside the figure. The generator of synthetic Minoan text, in Part Five, is available as a harder null than shuffling for anyone who wants one. **

What is not reproducible from this alone.** The figures of the Kanta and Civitillo papers are theirs; the Cretan Hieroglyphic corpus is published as photographs and is not machine-readable; and the transliteration of the ivory scepter is announced and not yet out.
